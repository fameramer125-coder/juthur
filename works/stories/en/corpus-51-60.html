One small thing we take with us, and one we leave on the shore.

51. The Pocket That Talks

الْجَيْبُ الَّذِي يَتَكَلَّمُ

With every step along the wide beach, something in Sindbad’s pocket said, “tick.” He did not notice. He was busy telling Kalila that the gulls above him were learning to fly from him, and his hand had been clutching his pocket since dawn. Talia, who hears the smallest sounds, turned to him and said, “Your pocket is talking.”

He put in his hand and drew out the smooth stone Ali had given him, the lemon seed Talia had given him, and Kalila’s whisker. Beneath them lay a piece of glass with a sharp edge, with grains of golden dust clinging to it. “I found it yesterday in a fold of my cloak,” he said. “From the glass forest!” He held it up to the sun, and it cast a trembling spot of light on the sand. “This is the proof of my adventure!” Kalila said, yawning, “Proof for whom? Everyone who knows the story is walking beside you.”

Talia laughed, and then noticed something else. She said, “Look behind you.” In the corner of his pocket was a small hole that no stone or seed could pass through, and golden dust was leaking out of it, grain by grain, drawing on the sand a thin thread that stretched far back behind them, with the waves licking at its end.

Sindbad stopped. He weighed the piece in his palm for a long while, the way a merchant weighs a coin of unknown value. It had scratched his finger twice since yesterday, and he had admitted it to no one. Then he walked to where the water reached and raised his arm to hurl it with all his strength. Kalila said, “The sea is not another pocket, Sindbad.”

His arm stopped in the air. He looked at the clear water at his feet and saw a small fish passing without fear, and he lowered his arm. Talia picked a dry palm leaf from the sand, and Sindbad wrapped the piece in it carefully, and they walked to the end of the beach, where a rusty drum stood with one word painted on it in white by the fishermen: Glass. Sindbad dropped the piece in. Talia heard one “tick” from below, and then nothing.

He walked back. And the hand that had been clutching his pocket since dawn swung loose at his side, until on the way it brushed against Talia’s hand.

Activity

Empty your pocket or your bag onto a table and line up what is in it. Choose one thing you have not used for a while, and decide where you want to put it. If you find something broken or sharp-edged, ask a grown-up to hold it with you and show you the right place to throw it away.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad find in his pocket, and what did he do with the piece of glass?
  2. What in your own bag is like this pocket?

52. The Stone That Was His Grandfather’s

الْحَجَرُ الَّذِي كَانَ لِجَدِّهِ

At a narrow stream strewn with flat stones, Ali stood hugging to his chest a round grey stone. It had been his grandfather’s, who used to warm his hands on it by the fire in winter and set it on a corner of the tent when the wind blew. The smell of damp moss rose from the water, the sun danced on its surface, and its sound changed from place to place.

Sindbad said, “Give me that stone of yours, and I’ll carry it with one finger!” Ali looked at him, then slowly held out the stone. Sindbad’s knees buckled, his face went red, and he said in a strangled voice, “It needs two fingers.” Kalila said, “And the rest of your body.” Ali took his stone back, hiding a smile, and Sindbad hopped over the flat stones one after another until he reached the far bank, and raised his arms as though he had won a race.

Then Ali began to cross. The stone in his arms hid his feet from him. He stepped onto the first flat stone, then the second, and at the third his sandal slipped and he froze where he stood. He stood in the middle of the stream with the cold water curling around his ankles. He did not put the stone in the water, and he did not ask for help, and he did not know where to set his next foot.

Talia stood on the near bank, listening to the stream, and said, “Where the water whispers there is a flat stone, and where it knocks there is a gap.” Sindbad called from the far bank, and this time said nothing about himself: “Left. Flat. Now the right. Hold still!” And Talia corrected from where she stood: “Whispering. Yes, here it whispers.”

Foot by foot, Ali arrived, his knees trembling with cold. He set the stone down on the sand, and a damp circle was printed beneath it. Sindbad sat beside him and did not say he was a hero. On the sand nearby, Ali’s deep footprints lay next to Sindbad’s light ones.

Activity

With a grown-up, lay five cushions or big sheets of paper on the floor like stepping stones in a stream, and clear away anything sharp. Carry a few light books so that they hide your feet from you, and let a friend guide you by voice from the other end. Then swap roles.

Questions for the guide

  1. What happened to Ali in the middle of the stream, and who helped him, and with what?
  2. Do you remember a time when someone helped you with their voice alone?

53. The Wind That Changed Its Mind

الرِّيحُ الَّتِي غَيَّرَتْ رَأْيَهَا

Kalila knew the wind the way people know their friends’ faces. She would wet the tip of her nose with her tongue and lift it, then say, “The wind is from the west, and the water is in the west.” She had never once been wrong, and Sindbad had come to ask her about the wind before he asked about breakfast. That morning she smelled in the air a smell like rain on stone, and she set off toward it, and they followed.

They walked behind her among the dunes for hours, with the sun rising and the sand scorching under their sandals, and the waterskin on Ali’s shoulder growing lighter and lighter. Then they came to a high wall of sand, and found behind it only more sand. Sindbad said, “This is what happens when we trust a nose!” Kalila did not answer. She sat down on the sand and wrapped her tail around her feet, and went on gazing into the distance as though something out there owed her an apology. Ali asked, “Shall we go back?” She said, “To where? The last well we passed was a whole day behind us.”

Sindbad wanted to run back and lead the way himself, and before he could open his mouth Talia said in a low voice, “In the morning the few grasses were bending toward us. Now they are turning their backs.”

Kalila lifted her ears. “So the wind did not betray me,” she said. “It only changed its mind, and I did not ask it again.” Ali sat beside her and poured a little water into his palm and offered it to her. Sindbad sighed, and sat down too, poking at the sand with a finger, counting its grains until he was tired of counting.

After a while Kalila wet her nose again. “North-east,” she said, “and there is the smell of mud in it.” Sindbad wet his finger with spit and raised it. He felt cold on one side of it, and swallowed his words. And so Kalila’s nose and Sindbad’s finger rose into the air, like two small flags asking the same question.

Activity

Go out with a grown-up to an open place, a yard or a garden, with a light strip of paper or cloth. Hold it in your hand and watch which way it leans. Then come back a little later and see whether it leans another way.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Kalila do after they reached the wall of sand, and what did Talia notice?
  2. What do you do when you find that the plan you were following no longer works?

54. The Tear Someone Saw

الدَّمْعَةُ الَّتِي رَآهَا أَحَدٌ

After supper Talia moved a few steps away from the fire and sat under a lote tree, her arms wrapped around her knees. The ground beneath her was cool and damp, and the leaves of the lote tree rubbed against one another. The firelight reached her, with the smell of smoke and the voices of her friends close by, and in the grass around her a cricket was trying to remember a tune. A bird whistled in the night, a long, hesitant whistle, and she remembered the whistle her grandfather used to call her with at the door of the house. A tear fell, then another, and she did not wipe them away, for she thought no one could see her.

Kalila was watching from a distance, with one ear tilted toward the tree. She got up and stretched like someone with other business, and passed by the tree as though by chance. She said, “Do you know? This is the best place to watch the moon, and I don’t like watching it alone.”

Talia wiped her cheek with her sleeve. “I’m not watching it.” Kalila said, “And I’m not asking you.” And she lay down beside her and rested her head against the trunk of the lote tree.

A long time passed, and the moon climbed through the branches. Then Talia said, “My grandfather used to call me with a whistle like that.” Kalila did not comment, and did not tell her to stop, or to be glad. She only said, “Tell me how he used to whistle.” And Talia told her, and imitated the whistle with trembling lips, and it came out like wind in a keyhole, and she gave a short laugh through her nose.

And Ali came, walking carefully, carrying two cups of warm milk with a small saucer on top of one. He put one cup beside Talia, and poured from his own cup into the saucer in front of Kalila, and said, “It’s hot. Careful.” Talia said, “Thank you, Ali,” and wrapped both hands around the cup, and the warmth passed into her cold fingers. He sat with them, holding the second cup. A thin steam rose from the cups and the saucer and wandered among the branches of the lote tree until it was lost in the moonlight.

Activity

Ask someone older whom you love to sit with you one evening and share something warm to drink. Tell them about a sound or a smell that reminds you of someone you miss. If you like, begin with a sentence such as: “He always used to...”

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Kalila do when she saw Talia crying, and what did Ali bring?
  2. If you miss someone, who would you like to sit beside you?

55. The Shadow That Came Back

الظِّلُّ الَّذِي عَادَ

At midday on a scorching day, they entered a small village whose houses were made of clay. The square was still, the smell of hot bread drifted from somewhere, and a lone hen pecked at the dirt. The air was so motionless that even the flies looked tired. Sindbad stood in the middle of the square and looked down, blinking in the glare, and then cried, “My shadow! My shadow has gone again!”

A woman leaned from a window, hanging out washing, and laughed. Sindbad ran around the well and looked under the bucket, and behind a wall, and under an overturned jar. Talia said, “Look at your feet.” Under his sandals was a small dark patch like a puddle. He said, “That’s not my shadow. Mine is longer.”

Kalila asked, “And when was it long?” “In the morning.” “And where was the sun in the morning?” “Over there, near the horizon.” “And now?” He lifted his head to look, and she said, “Don’t look at it. Let the ground answer.”

Ali drew a mark with his finger at Sindbad’s feet, and set a pebble at the tip of the small shadow. Then they sat under a roof of palm fronds by the wall, eating dates and watching the dust. After a while the woman came down with a jar of cold water, and poured a cup for each of them, and sat down to share the watching. And every time a while passed, Ali called, “Back to your mark!” and Sindbad stood on it like a statue, trying for the first time in his life not to move, and Ali set a new pebble at the tip of the shadow.

The pebbles moved away from his feet one after another, in a slanting line like a string of beads. Sindbad said, “It never left. It was only waiting under my feet.” And by late afternoon his shadow had stretched out its head across the dirt until it touched the shadow of Talia, who stood by the wall. Sindbad cried, “My shadow went to say hello to its neighbour!” And the two shadows shook hands.

Activity

On a sunny day, stand in a square or a yard and set a pebble at the tip of your shadow, and ask a friend to do the same. Come back every hour and set a new pebble. Never look straight at the sun. Watch the ground only.

Questions for the guide

  1. Where did Sindbad look for his shadow, and what did Ali notice from the movement of the pebbles?
  2. When do you think your shadow is shortest, and when is it longest?

An edge, and below it a bridge we have not yet seen.

56. The Deep Cleft

الشَّقُّ الْعَمِيقُ

After two days of walking, they stood before a deep cleft in the mountain, its walls red and its floor drowned in shadow, with a single eagle circling above it without moving its wings. The wind that rose from it was cold and carried the smell of stone wet with ancient water, and their voices came back to them late.

Ali stood well back from the edge and dropped a pebble into the cleft. Talia listened: “Tak. Then silence. Then a drop in water.” Sindbad said, “I can jump it!” Kalila said, “Of course. And the landing?” He found no answer, and sat on a stone scratching his head.

They walked along the cleft, several paces back from its rim, with Talia listening to the water below. She said, “Its sound grows thinner here. The cleft is narrowing.” At a place no wider than two tall men lying end to end, they found an old palm trunk, swept there by a flood long ago, lying on the ground.

Ali took the heavy end on his shoulder, and Sindbad took the other end, grumbling, while Talia called, “Closer. Closer. Enough.” The trunk settled on the two edges with a dull thud, and trembled a little and went still, and Ali pushed it with his shoulder and it did not move. Then Kalila tied to Sindbad’s waist one end of the rope she had once thrown down to the mirror pit, and Ali wound the other end around the trunk and held it with both hands.

Sindbad insisted on crossing first. He took two proud steps, then sat on the trunk and crawled, staring only at the wood and not once at what lay below, saying, “This is a new way of walking.” On the far side he untied the rope from his waist and fastened it to a rock, so that it became a taut handrail. Then Talia crossed with her hand on the rope, then Ali, and Kalila last, saying, “I don’t like walking on something that yawns.”

And behind them the rope swung a little in the wind, warm from four hands.

Activity

Draw a narrow cleft with chalk or masking tape on the floor of a room, and build a bridge over it from books and a ruler. Try sending a small toy across it. What happens if you move the two edges farther apart?

Questions for the guide

  1. How did they know where the cleft narrowed, and what did each of them do so that they could all cross?
  2. If you had been there, which job would you have liked to take?

57. The New Scarf

الْوِشَاحُ الْجَدِيدُ

They sat on the far side of the cleft, catching their breath. The rocks were warm from the sun, the smell of wormwood came from the slope, and Kalila was licking a paw that the gravel had hurt. Sindbad lay on his back and said he had not been afraid for a moment, while his legs were still trembling. Ali had scraped the inside of his palm on the rough bark of the trunk, and was hiding his hand under his arm as though nothing had happened. But Talia heard his breath catch whenever he closed his fingers.

She stood up and looked around, and saw on a thorn bush a blue scarf embroidered with silver threads, snagged on the thorns like a bird that had lost its way. She called, “Whose scarf is this?” and the deep cleft answered, “Scarf... scarf...” She called again, and no one answered but the echo.

Kalila sniffed the scarf and said, “It smells of a house, not of a mountain. If nobody asks for it, perhaps one of us needs it.” Talia looked at Ali’s hand. She freed the scarf carefully from the thorns, shook the dust from it, and sat down in front of him. She said, “Show me your hand.” He said, “There’s nothing wrong with it.” She said, “Then it won’t hurt for me to look.” He opened his palm reluctantly, and she saw a red line and a fine splinter of bark. She removed it with the edge of her nail and poured a little water from the waterskin over it. He said, “The scarf will get dirty.” She said, “It’s a scarf, not a museum.” And she wound it twice around his hand and tied it at the wrist, neither tight nor loose. Ali moved his fingers, and his breath came out free. She said, “Does it hurt?” He said, “Less.”

Sindbad took from his belt the piece of cloth that had been his grandmother’s, its colour faded with the years, and laid it beside the new one. “The same blue,” he said, “but this one shines more.” Kalila laughed. “And that one has more stories.”

And Sindbad put the old cloth back in his belt. And Ali raised his bandaged hand toward the sun to try it, and the silver threads flashed.

Activity

Borrow a piece of cloth from your family and ask them to show you how to tie a knot that holds but does not squeeze. Practise on a doll, or, gently and with a grown-up beside you, on a friend’s forearm, then untie it slowly.

Questions for the guide

  1. What was Ali hiding, and how did Talia know?
  2. If you found something that was not yours and saw that someone near you needed it, what would you do with it?

58. Kalila’s Rock

صَخْرَةُ كَلِيلَةَ

Just before sunset Kalila reached her rock on the rocky shore, smooth-faced and warm from the day’s sun, with old scratches at one end from her claws. The smell of seaweed rose around it, and the waves broke on the distant rocks. She found that Sindbad had got there first and was sitting on it, shelling a walnut. She said nothing. She sat down on the sand beside the rock and gazed at the sky.

“Why are you looking up?” he asked. “I’m waiting.” “For what?” “A guest.” “When does it come?” “When it comes.” Five minutes later he asked, “Now?” And five more: “Now?” And he kept tapping the rock with a walnut shell. Ali and Talia came, looked at Kalila and then at the sky, and sat down in silence. Talia offered them dates, and they took them without a word, while the sea went from blue to grey.

Suddenly Talia raised her hand. “Listen.” Sindbad heard nothing. Then a delicate sound came from far away, the beating of many wings, approaching as rain approaches. And in the orange sky a long line appeared in the shape of an arrowhead, a flock of cranes heading south. Each bird was calling to the one ahead. Kalila said softly, “They pass this way every autumn, and they never land.” And Ali raised his hand and waved to the flock as one waves to a passing caravan.

Kalila’s eye glistened. Sindbad said in a low voice, “Are you crying?” She said, “It’s the salt. The sea sprinkles its salt on everyone.” And Sindbad fell silent. He gathered the walnut shells around him into his palm so as not to leave a mess on her rock, then shuffled to the end of the rock and patted an empty place beside him, and Kalila sprang up and settled into it.

And when the last of the birds had vanished over the horizon, Kalila stretched out a forepaw and patted the rock twice, the way someone does who is promising to return.

Activity

Go with a grown-up to an open place shortly before sunset and sit waiting to see a single bird. Count what passes and listen to its sounds. If no bird passes, draw what you thought you heard.

Questions for the guide

  1. What was Kalila waiting for at her rock, and what did Sindbad do after her eye glistened?
  2. Do you have a place where you like to sit and wait for beautiful things?

59. The Last Stone

آخِرُ حَجَرٍ

Sindbad tried to spread out a map Talia had drawn, on top of a small hill with nothing on it but one rock. The evening was calm, the light poured golden over the hill, and the smell of wild thyme rose from the grass. The map blew out of his hand, and Ali caught it and laid his grandfather’s stone on it, as he always did. As soon as the wind dropped, he picked up the stone again.

Talia noticed that he kept switching the stone from one arm to the other, and rubbing his shoulder, and that his forehead gleamed with sweat, while grasshoppers sang in the grass around them. Sindbad said, “Why don’t you put it down for a moment?” After a while Ali said, “I thought whoever carries doesn’t need anyone.” He looked at the stone as if saying goodbye to it. “It has been heavy for weeks.”

Kalila said, “The stone won’t be angry if we take turns with it.” Ali said nothing. Then his hand shook a little as he held the stone out toward Sindbad. “Take it. Your turn.”

Sindbad received it in his arms, and a long groan escaped him and his back bent, and he said in broken breaths, “With one finger... isn’t that right?” Ali laughed, a broad laugh that no one had heard from him before, and Kalila said, “And the rest of the body, I think.” Sindbad said, “I carried a purse of dinars and an old chest, and this is heavier than both together.” And he tried to take three steps, and swayed as if dancing with a camel.

Ali’s arms swung in the air, not knowing where to put themselves. He sat down and rolled his shoulders in a full circle, and said as though to himself, “The air is lighter than I thought.” Talia held out a date to him without a word, and he took it and ate it. And they sat around the map, and Talia pointed with a finger to the glass forest at the far corner of the paper. And when the map blew away again, he did not look at the stone. He laid his empty palm on its corner to hold it down, and the stone rested in Sindbad’s lap.

Activity

Choose with a grown-up something a little heavy, like a basket of fruit, and carry it together from one room to another, each of you with one hand. Then try carrying it alone, and compare the two times.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Talia notice about Ali, and what did he do with the stone in the end?
  2. Have you ever carried something alone and then had someone offer to share it with you?

60. Where the Road Ended and the Crossing Began

حَيْثُ انْتَهَى الطَّرِيقُ وَبَدَأَ الْعُبُورُ

Dawn was breathing a light chill when they reached the place where the journey had begun: the glass forest. The trees rang in the breeze as though someone were blowing a flute far away, dew wetted the grass, and the golden dust shone beneath their feet. Around the rim of the mirror pit, grass had grown.

Sindbad stood at the edge with Ali’s stone in his arms. He said, “This is where I used to shout.” Kalila said, “And now?” He did not answer. He sat on the ground and waited until the sun was fully up and warming their backs, the longest he had ever waited for anything.

Talia took the shell from her pocket and held it to his ear. He heard the sea at once, without waiting an hour. Ali sat beside him and held the end of the stone, the blue scarf still around his hand. Sindbad said, “It never fell from me once.” Ali said, “I know. I knew it since the mud. Take it with you until we meet.”

From far away came a long, faint call, and Kalila pricked up her ears, then rose and shook out her tail. “The cranes have gone south ahead of me,” she said. And Sindbad asked, quietly this time, “Will we see you?” She said, “Ask the wind. And ask it twice.” He scratched his nose as he had on the day of the pit, then said, “If you please... come back.” She said, “That is the finest ‘if you please’ I have heard from you. And this time it came without a rope.”

She laid her forehead against his hand, and Talia touched Kalila’s ear with a fingertip, one touch. Then Kalila walked in among the glass trees, and the trees rang as her tail brushed them. And Talia went on listening to the fall of Kalila’s paws until it faded.

Then each went their own way, Sindbad toward the north, Talia toward the sea, Ali toward the desert. And behind Sindbad, on the forest floor, a thin golden thread trickled from the hole in his pocket, glinting and then fading with the distance.

Activity

Sit with a grown-up and open the book at the first story. Choose a picture or a sentence that stayed with you, and draw it. Then draw beside it something from the last story, and see how the two differ.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad do in the forest this time that was different from what he did there in the first story?
  2. Which of these four do you feel closest to today?
