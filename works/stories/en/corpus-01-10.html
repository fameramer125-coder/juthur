Section 1 · At the Start of the Road

A bag on the shoulder, and a mirror that shows its owner more than he wanted.

1. Kalila and the Mirror of Pride

كَلِيلَةُ وَمِرْآةُ الْكِبْرِيَاءِ

The trees in that forest were made of glass, and whenever the wind passed through them their branches rang like small bells. The dust was fine and shone like gold powder. But Sindbad heard nothing and looked at nothing, because his purse of dinars was lost. He circled among the trunks, shouting, “Who took my purse? I am Sindbad the Sailor, master of seven voyages!”

Kalila stepped out from behind a clear trunk, licking her paw. “Seven voyages,” she said, “and you still don’t look at your feet.” Sindbad waved a hand as if he were shooing a fly. “Go away! I have no time for foxes!” He took two steps, and the ground gave way beneath him.

He fell into a pit, not a deep one, but its walls were polished glass, like mirrors. Wherever he turned he saw an angry, scowling Sindbad opening and closing his mouth. He shouted, and every mirror shouted with him. He pushed a wall, and another Sindbad pushed back from the other side. Then he ran his palm along the glass and found it cold and smooth, with nothing for his fingers to hold. “Where is the door? They are all me!”

Kalila peered over the edge. “Be quiet for a moment. There is one wall that doesn’t show you your face.” Sindbad fell silent, grudgingly. In that quiet he heard the tap of her claw on the glass, and among all the faces he saw a rectangle of blue sky with no face in it.

She threw him a rope. He caught it, then hesitated, scratched his nose, and said in a voice that did not sound like a captain’s, “If you please... would you hold the other end?” “My rope only tightens for polite words,” said Kalila, and she wound it around a trunk.

When he was out, he found his purse hanging from a glass branch, where it had snagged as he ran. He took it, drew out one dinar and set it on the rock beside Kalila. She sniffed it. “You can’t eat that.” Even so, she carried it off in her mouth and buried it under the trunk as if it were a bone. Before they left he glanced back at the pit, and in the very last mirror he saw a small Sindbad waving to him. This time he was smiling.

Activity

Stand in front of a mirror and try three faces: an angry one, then a calm one, then whichever face you choose yourself. Afterwards, draw a wall of mirrors with a gap in it where the sky comes through.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad notice in the wall that did not show him his face?
  2. What would you do if you found yourself in a pit whose walls were mirrors?

2. The Rope of Trust

حَبْلُ الثِّقَةِ

At the river the water tumbled over pebbles, and the smell of damp moss filled the air. Above it stood a bridge of old planks, with a rope stretched beside it between two posts. Sindbad stopped at the first plank, tightening his belt. “I crossed the seven seas. Should I be afraid of a little river?” But his foot would not move, and his knees trembled for a reason he could not see.

Kalila leapt onto the bridge and walked to the middle, then sat down to wait for him, arranging her tail. “At sea a ship carries you. Here one plank carries you, and then another.” “These planks creak!” he said. “They will break.” “Try one plank,” she said. “Not the whole bridge.”

Sindbad took hold of the rope and found it fastened with a sailor’s knot, a tight one he knew well. He felt it with his thumb: two turns and a loop, pulled firm as though it had grown into the post. He hauled on it with all his strength and it did not budge. He smiled a small smile, because a hand that knew its knots had come this way before him.

He stepped onto the first plank and it creaked under his foot, and he froze. He heard the water laughing on the pebbles below and smelled the moss rising to meet him. Then he stepped onto the second and the third, counting aloud, with the river beneath him too shallow to reach his knees. At the seventh plank something shifted under him, and he gripped the rope. Kalila asked from where she sat, “Which plank comes next?” So he tested the one ahead with the toe of his boot, and then moved his weight onto it.

On the far bank he shook out his robe and turned around. The bridge had seventeen planks, no more. “Seventeen!” he said. “And I took it for a sea!” Then he looked about as though afraid someone had overheard. Kalila laughed until her whiskers shook.

Then he walked to the post where the rope was tied, made a second knot over the old one, and pulled it tight. He patted the post the way you pat a friend’s shoulder.

Activity

Lay a rope or a long ribbon on level ground and walk along it step by step, counting. Try once with quick steps and once with slow ones, and tell whoever is with you which was steadier.

Questions for the guide

  1. How did Sindbad cross the bridge, and what did he do when the plank shook?
  2. Has something ever looked very big to you and then grown smaller once you began it?

3. The Stubborn Chest

الصُّنْدُوقُ الْعَنِيدُ

Sindbad found the chest half buried in the sand, with a thin film of morning dew on its lid. “My treasure!” he cried. He picked it up and spun around once to show it to the sea, and found that it was heavy, with no lock and no keyhole.

He struck it with a stone, and a hollow sound came back: thoop, thoop. He kicked it, which hurt his foot, and hopped about on one leg, rubbing the toes of the other. Then he stood in front of it and spoke to it. “Open in the name of the sea! Open in the name of the captain!” The chest did nothing.

Kalila was sitting in the shade, nibbling a date. “Do you want my opinion? Wood swells with the dew and shrinks in the sun. And you are hitting it before it has woken up.” Sindbad did not quite understand, but his shoulders ached from carrying the chest and beating it, so he sat down beside it and took a date from her.

An hour went by. Kalila lined up two date stones in the sand. The sun climbed, the sand grew warm, and the smell of wet wood turned into the smell of warm wood. Suddenly he heard a small sound: tak. He looked, and on the side of the lid a line as fine as a hair had appeared. He pushed the lid with one finger and it slid sideways, easily, as though it had never been shut.

Inside lay a folded square of blue cloth, and under it a roll of leather with lines carved into it. No gold, no pearls. Sindbad raised his eyebrows. “Is this all?” He turned the rolled leather over in his hands and could make nothing of its lines. Kalila did not answer, because her mouth was full of dates.

Then he sniffed the cloth, and it smelled of cinnamon and sandalwood, as though someone had folded it with love. He laid it over his shoulder like a banner. He took the last date from his pocket and set it in the bottom of the empty chest. “One for you,” he said. “You were patient with me.” He slid the lid back, and it glided into place with a soft sound: tak.

Activity

Make a small chest out of stiff card, with a lid that slides sideways instead of lifting. Put something simple inside, give it to someone in your family, and let them discover for themselves how it opens.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad do before he sat down, and what did he notice after he sat?
  2. Have you ever tried to open something by force, and it opened some other way?

4. The Map You Cannot Read Sitting Down

الْخَرِيطَةُ الَّتِي لَا تُقْرَأُ جَالِسًا

A circle with a dot in the middle, three wavy lines, and a broken triangle: that was all there was on the leather Sindbad spread across his knees. He turned it over and upside down and held it up to his nose as if to smell it. “This is a language no sailor ever made!” he said.

Kalila said, “You read a road while sitting down? Get up. Hold the leather up against the land, and tell me what looks like what.” He did not understand, but he stood.

He headed for the first triangular hill he saw and climbed it, panting, under a merciless sun, only to find its summit smooth, with no break in it. From the bottom Kalila called, “A triangle, yes. But is it broken?” He came down brushing sand from his robe, and this time he walked with his eyes on the leather and on the land together.

He walked until he came to a rock split into two halves, and he looked at the leather: the broken triangle! Then came three leaning palms beside a narrow stream whose murmur he heard before he saw it: the wavy lines! Each time he shouted, “Here! This is it!” while Kalila ran ahead of him asking, “And how did you know this time? From the leather, or from your feet?”

The sun set, and a cool breeze came carrying the smell of wild mint. His feet were tired, and the sand under his soles had turned soft, then damp. At the last mark, the circle with the dot, he found a small spring welling up between two stones, with the glint of a star trembling in it. He washed his face and drank until he was satisfied, and then he laughed, for the dot in the middle of the circle on the leather was the very dot of the star in the water. Kalila said, lapping, “And you were the one who said it was a language no sailor ever made.”

Before he slept, worn out by the walk, he folded the map and did not put it in his pocket. He tucked it under a flat stone by the spring and left one corner sticking out, for whoever came after him to see.

Activity

Hide something small in your home and draw a map for someone in your family, using symbols of your own invention: a circle for the table, a triangle for the bed, or whatever you like. Then walk through the house together and see how the map matches the rooms.

Questions for the guide

  1. Which symbol did Sindbad find on the ground first, and what did he find at the last symbol?
  2. What would you draw if you made a map for a traveller who comes after you?

5. The Well That Remembers

الْبِئْرُ الَّتِي تَتَذَكَّرُ

At noon on the third day they came to a stone well beside an abandoned village, with a creaking pulley over it and a leather bucket. The stone was hot, but the air that rose from the mouth of the well was cool and touched the face. Along its rim ran smooth grooves worn by an old rope, as if a thousand hands had drawn water there. Between the stones a little thyme bush had grown, and its scent rose when Kalila’s tail brushed it.

Sindbad leaned over the opening and shouted into the depth, “Hoo-hoo!” His voice came back twice, slow and heavy, and then the depth went still. He called again without thinking, “Drink, my boy, before you spill half the bucket on your feet!” Then he stopped short. His grandfather’s sentence had come out of his mouth, in his grandfather’s very tone.

He said hoarsely, looking down into the dark, “That was his sentence. He said it every time at our own well, while I spilled half the bucket over my feet, and he laughed. I used to think he was talking to the well.” Kalila sat down beside him and said nothing, and laid her tail across his foot. A minute passed, with no sound but the creak of the pulley.

After a while she said, “The bucket is tied with a loose knot. Can you tie it properly?” He smiled between two tears and tried to tie it the way he tied the ropes of ships, but the knot slipped out of his hand. Then he loosened his fingers and let them search on their own, and they came to the first knot his grandfather had ever taught him: a turn, a turn, a loop, a pull. Then he lowered the bucket. They waited, and from below they heard a soft thud and then the gurgle of water filling the bucket.

The bucket came up full, dripping cold water. Sindbad raised his eyes to the sky and said, “May God have mercy on him.” Then he drank from his cupped hands; the water was colder than he had expected, and sweeter. He held the bucket out to Kalila, and she dipped in her tongue, and a drop fell from her chin onto the hot stone and vanished in steam.

Activity

Ask one of the elders in your family to teach you a knot they know well, then tie it yourself three times. If you like, begin your story to them with a sentence such as: “I remember that my hands know...”

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad hear when he called into the well, and what did Kalila do?
  2. What is something your hands know because someone taught you?

6. Antara and the Distant Sound

عَنْتَرَةُ وَالصَّوْتُ الْبَعِيدُ

The heat grew so fierce that the sand stung feet right through their sandals. In the shade of a lone rock sat a broad-shouldered horseman, his mare beside him and his spear planted in the sand. His name was Antara, and he was listening.

Sindbad arrived panting. “Horseman! Which way to the oasis? I am Sindbad the Sailor, and I wait for no one!” Antara did not turn. Nothing about him moved except the end of his turban in the wind. Sindbad shouted louder, “Can you hear me?” Antara only raised his palm, like a man saying, “Give me a moment.”

Kalila whispered, “Lower your voice. He is listening to something.” Sindbad sat down, grumbling, and wiped the sweat away with his sleeve. His ears filled with the whistle of the wind over the sand, and then, from very far away, came something thin: ding... ding... Antara rose and said only two words: “Lost kid.” Then he walked.

Sindbad followed him. “I didn’t hear anything!” “You were busy with your talking,” said Kalila. Antara stopped every ten paces and tilted his head, and his mare turned her ears toward the same side, so that Sindbad began to stop as they did and tilt his head, while Kalila hid her laughter behind her paw.

In a crack between two rocks they found a young goat, a little kid, whose hair had snagged on a thorn bush, with a copper bell at its neck. Antara freed it with big, gentle fingers, strand by strand. Then he stroked its head with his palm, and the little one trembled for a moment and went still. Sindbad picked it up, and soft hairs tickled his chin, and the bell rang close to his ear: ding, ding. “I rescued him!” he said proudly. No one argued with him, and Antara smiled with only the corner of his mouth.

When he wanted to ask once more about the oasis, he opened his mouth and shut it again. From far off came the bleating of a whole flock, all from one direction, and in that direction the green of palms shone above the dunes. Sindbad tilted his head toward it, and kept it tilted until he had carried the kid back to its mother.

Activity

Work with a grown-up. They hide somewhere in the house and tap a cup lightly with a spoon. Stand still where you are with your eyes open, point toward the place the sound comes from, and then walk to it.

Questions for the guide

  1. What was Antara listening to, and what did Sindbad do so that he could hear it too?
  2. What did you hear once when you went quiet for a moment?

7. Yasmina and the Nightmare That Never Came Back

يَاسْمِينَا وَالْكَابُوسُ الَّذِي لَمْ يَعُدْ

Every dawn Yasmina woke with her heart pounding, in her small house at the edge of the sea. She would tell her mother, “I saw a black hand reaching toward me.” Her mother would hold her, light the little lamp and stroke her hair, and they would listen to the sound of the waves until she grew calm. In the daytime she played on the beach with shells and remembered nothing about the hand.

Two guests came to stay in the house: a sailor who snored in his sleep, and a fox who claimed to be a detective. When Kalila heard the story she said, “Where does this hand begin? Let’s wait for it together at dawn.”

The next dawn Yasmina sat up in bed, with her mother, Kalila and Sindbad beside her, and the lamp giving off a faint glow. They waited, with the air smelling of salt and the waves sighing beneath the window. Then, as the edge of the sky turned white, a long black hand with five fingers slid across the wall and began to sway slowly. Yasmina pulled the blanket up to her chin, and her mother gave her hand one small squeeze.

Kalila said quietly, “Let’s follow it.” Yasmina took her mother’s hand, and her mother whispered, “My hand is in yours.” They crept on tiptoe to the window. At the glass she saw a long palm frond tapping at the pane: tik, tik. The dawn light stood behind it and threw its shadow on the wall, with five swaying fingers.

Sindbad opened his mouth to laugh, but Yasmina’s mother gave him one look and he closed it, and whispered, “Just a palm frond.” Yasmina touched the glass with a finger where the frond tapped, and felt the light tap beneath her finger. Kalila asked her, “Is it this long every dawn?” and Yasmina nodded.

In the morning her father set up a ladder, humming an old tune, and tied the frond back with a rope, away from the glass. At the next dawn Yasmina woke without her heart pounding. She sat looking at the wall and saw the shadow of the hand swaying far off, and she raised her own hand and waved to it.

Activity

With a grown-up, switch on a flashlight in a slightly dim room and make a shadow on the wall with your hand. Change your fingers until the shadow becomes a rabbit, then a bird, then whatever you choose, and let the others guess.

Questions for the guide

  1. Where did the black hand Yasmina saw on the wall come from?
  2. Has a shadow ever looked strange to you in the dark, and then you recognised it when daylight came?

8. Baydaba and the Gate That Never Opened

بَيْدَبَا وَالْبَوَّابَةُ الَّتِي لَمْ تُفْتَحْ

A high wall of black stone blocked the road, and in its middle stood a huge wooden gate, studded with nails and carved with old inscriptions. In its shade sat a thin old man with a white beard, blowing on a copper cup of tea. His name was Baydaba.

“Open for us!” said Sindbad, and he shoved the gate with his shoulder. It did not budge. He pounded it with his fist and the whole place boomed. He read the inscriptions aloud, and nothing changed. Then he walked around the wall looking for a second gate, and found only black stone running on as far as he could see. Baydaba sipped his tea and said, “The shoulder is big, and the door is bigger.” Then he fell silent.

Sindbad grew angry. He plopped onto the ground and folded his arms across his chest. And because there was nothing left to do, he looked. Low in the wall, near his knees, he saw a little door that did not reach his waist, hidden behind a cactus, with a handle made of string. The smell that seeped through its crack was the smell of ripe figs.

“A children’s door?” he said. Baydaba did not answer, but raised his cup toward the little door as if greeting an old friend. Kalila said, “I went in yesterday.” Before he could ask her anything, her tail had vanished through the opening. He called after her, “And why would anyone hide a door like that?” Her voice came back from inside: “No one hid it. You were only standing on top of it.”

Sindbad hesitated. Bend down? He? Then he pulled the string, and the door opened with a faint creak. As he stooped to go in, his turban fell from his head. He caught it, held it in his hand, and went in crawling on his hands and knees.

Inside lay a whole orchard: figs and pomegranates, and cool shade on the grass. Sindbad stood up, dusted off his knees, set his turban back on his head, a little crooked, and looked behind him. Baydaba had put his empty cup on the threshold of the great gate, which was shut just as before, and he was laughing without a sound.

Activity

With a grown-up’s help, cut a small door out of cardboard for a doll or a paper animal, and make it low enough that the doll must bend to go through. Then tie a string handle to it and hide it behind something in the room for a grown-up to find.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad try at the big gate, and what did he see once he sat down?
  2. Do you remember a small door or passage in a place you know that stayed hidden while you were looking for a big way in?

9. Pictures on the Water

صُوَرٌ عَلَى سَطْحِ الْمَاءِ

Night fell on the courtyard of an old house. In its middle stood a round stone basin with a low rim, and in its water a great moon was reflected. The air was cool, the scent of jasmine filled the courtyard, the crickets never stopped, and Kalila sat on the rim scratching her ear.

Sindbad knelt at the rim and looked. In the water he saw what was not in the sky: a ship rocking with the waves, his father’s face smiling, a boy running along a quay, a laugh he had heard years ago. His chest tightened, and he reached out to catch the ship.

The water shattered into circles and everything vanished. All that was left in his palm was a cold wetness, and his sleeve was soaked. He tried again, and the circles grew. Kalila said, dipping her whisker, “Water can’t be held, Sindbad. Put your hands on your knees and look.”

Sindbad bit his lip, then set his hands on his knees and waited. His hands wanted to move, and he pressed on his knees until they settled. Little by little the circles calmed. The moon came back, then the ship, but this time it floated with its sails spread. Then it slid slowly to the side of the basin and the shadow wiped it away, and in its place appeared a picture he had never seen before: a small boy sitting on a quay, eating a fig. Sindbad laughed. “I had forgotten this one!” Kalila rested her chin on the rim and said, “Tell me about this one.” So he told her about the fig, and the seller who sang while he weighed the fruit, and she listened with her ears pricked.

The night went on like that: one picture coming, one going, and he sat without reaching out. At dawn, when the moon had paled and Kalila was asleep, a sparrow landed on the rim and drank. The water shivered and all the pictures dissolved at once, and Sindbad simply raised his eyes to the sparrow.

When he got up his sleeve was still wet, and he did not wring it out.

Activity

With a grown-up, pour water into a deep dish on a steady table and look into it toward the light of a window or a lamp. Stir it with one finger, then put your hands in your lap and watch what happens to the picture.

Questions for the guide

  1. What happened to the pictures when Sindbad reached out, and what happened when he put his hands on his knees?
  2. Which old picture would you like to see again, and who would you like to tell about it?

10. The Silence That Spoke

الصَّمْتُ الَّذِي تَكَلَّمَ

The last thing Sindbad found in the forest was a flat, polished rock with a line carved on it: “Here a traveller sat and listened.” He read it twice, ran his thumb over the cold letters, and walked around the rock looking for the letters of his own name. “Where is my name? I thought they would carve my name!”

Kalila said, stretching out on the grass, “There is no room for names on it. This rock is for ears. Sit down, and count every sound you hear. I doubt you will reach ten.” Sindbad lifted his nose. “Ten? I will count twenty!” And he sat. The rock was warm from the morning sun, and the smell of damp grass rose around it. “One!” he said aloud, and Kalila whispered, “In your chest only.”

Only two at first: the murmur of a far-off brook, and a woodpecker knocking on a trunk: tok, tok, tok. Then three more: rustling leaves, the hum of a bee, and a loud rumble that came from his own belly. His face went red, and he scratched his ear as though nothing had happened. Kalila said, “Count it. It is an honest sound.” She shut one eye and watched him with the other.

Six, seven, eight... He heard what he had not heard once in seven voyages: a pine cone falling on the dirt, the creak of a moving branch, and his own breathing, in and out. Then came the ninth: the coo of a far-off dove. And when he reached the tenth, which was the beat of his own heart in his ear, he stopped counting without noticing.

A green beetle landed on his knee and walked across it carefully, and he breathed slowly so as not to frighten it. He did not flick it off. He followed it with his eyes until it reached the edge of his robe and flew away, and he heard, for the first time, the whirr of its two tiny wings.

Kalila said, “How many did you count?” Sindbad said quietly, “I forgot.” Then he looked at the line for a long time, and at the empty place beneath it. He picked up a sharp pebble from the grass, hesitated, and slowly, patiently, carved beneath the old line a small ear, with no name in it.

Activity

Go out into a garden or onto a balcony with a grown-up and sit for two minutes with a large sheet of paper and a pen in your lap. Draw a line or a shape for each sound you hear, making a near sound a big drawing and a far sound a small one.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad hear while he sat on the rock, and what did he do with the pebble in the end?
  2. What might we hear in our home if we sat quietly for two minutes?
