41. The Road Only Walkers Can See

الطَّرِيقُ لَا يَرَاهُ إِلَّا مَنْ مَشَى عَلَيْهِ

Sindbad sat on a flat rock after the sun had gone down behind the hills. Grey stones stretched around him in every direction, the smell of dry grass filled the air, the cold wind nipped at his ears, and his stomach growled with hunger.

“The compass is lost,” he said aloud, as if the hills might apologise. “And the road is lost with it. I can’t see anything.”

Talia sat down beside him and listened. Kalila stood on a rock higher than both of them and swished her tail.

She said, “You’ve been looking for an hour. What have you found?”

“Stones.”

“A respectable discovery. Now get up and walk to that thorn bush.”

“Why? There’s no path to it!”

“Try. And if I’m wrong, you may say to me, ‘Didn’t I tell you so?’”

Sindbad hesitated. Sitting was easier, and his rock was a little warm from his body. But he hated the thought of Kalila laughing at him.

He got up, grumbling, and took a step. The gravel crunched under his sandal with a rough sound. He took a second step, and the sound turned soft, and the ground turned firm and packed, as if many feet had trodden it before him.

He stopped. “The ground is different here.”

Talia said, “And the sound of your steps here is not the sound of them there. I can hear the difference.”

Sindbad walked on, and whenever his foot strayed from the hard ground, Talia called him back to it with her voice. Little by little a pale ribbon appeared, winding among the rocks, with old goat droppings on it. Talia knelt, touching its edge with her fingertips and smelling the warm dust that rose from it.

Kalila said, hiding a laugh, “A goat track. It has been waiting for years for someone to walk on it.”

Sindbad looked back at his rock. It was only ten paces away. “I was looking for it with my eyes, and it was under my feet!”

Then he took off his sandal and shook out a small pebble that had been hurting him since noon. He stared at it and said, “And this I hadn’t seen, either.”

Kalila laughed until she coughed.

Activity

Go out with a grown-up to a garden or a safe yard. Walk slowly over three kinds of ground, such as grass, dirt and paving, and listen to the sound of each step. Then tell your companion what you noticed with your feet.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad and Talia notice when Sindbad began to walk?
  2. Has something ever been close to you, and you noticed it only late?

42. The Bell That Would Not Ring

الْجَرَسُ الَّذِي لَا يَرِنُّ

In the village square a large copper bell hung over the well, with a rope dangling from it, and it had not rung for two winters. Children struck it with sticks and it gave out a muffled sound like a wet drum, and the old baker woman would say, “The bell is dead.”

Sindbad arrived at noon with dust on his sandals. The heat was fierce, the smell of bread from the oven crept between the houses, and the chickens pecked the dirt under the bell as if they did not care. He heard the story and rolled up his sleeves. “I mend what time has broken.” He took a big stone and struck, and the sound came out muffled. He struck harder, and the sound grew more muffled. His face turned red and sweat ran down his forehead.

Kalila said, “Did you listen properly? I mean to the bell’s sound, not the sound of your blow.”

Sindbad said, “What’s the difference?”

“Your blow is loud, and the bell’s sound is muffled. So why would it be muffled, I wonder?”

Sindbad sat under the bell, puffing and blowing. Talia lifted her head and said, “Something is hanging down from inside it.”

A strand of dry straw hung from the mouth of the bell like a mouse’s tail. Sindbad called the baker woman, who brought a wooden ladder and held it steady with both hands. He climbed two rungs only, reached up, and drew out the straw gently.

Out came a whole nest of mud and straw, which a swallow had wedged between the bell’s clapper and its wall before leaving it behind. Kalila looked at the nest and said, “Don’t throw it away. Perhaps its owner will come back in spring.” So Talia set the nest on a beam of the well’s roof, away from the bell.

Then Sindbad pulled the rope gently. The bell rang with a long, deep note, and the whole square shook, and many heads looked out of windows. The children stood around the well with their mouths open, and the smallest clapped.

Sindbad came down the ladder with the ringing still trembling in his chest. The baker woman put a hot loaf in his hand, and he found not one word in his pocket to boast with.

Activity

Sit still with a grown-up in a quiet room and tap a glass gently with a spoon. Listen to the ringing until it fades away completely, and raise your hand when you can no longer hear it.

Questions for the guide

  1. What was really keeping the bell silent?
  2. Do you have a toy or something at home that does not work, and how do we find out why?

43. A Snail Draws Its Path in the Dark

حَلَزُونٌ يَرْسُمُ طَرِيقَهُ فِي الظَّلَامِ

The three stopped in a dense forest where the moonlight did not reach the ground. They had wandered away from the camp, where the grown-ups were waiting for them, to gather firewood, and now they no longer knew where it was. The smell of damp earth filled their noses, the dark was so thick that Sindbad could not see his own hand, and the crickets shrilled around them without pause.

Sindbad said, “You are the one, Kalila, who said, ‘Take a shortcut!’”

She said, “And you are the one who said, ‘I know this forest like the back of my hand.’”

He said, “And can you see the back of my hand now?”

Talia tripped over something and knelt, feeling the ground carefully. A small snail was crawling among the leaves, and behind it ran a thin silver line that gleamed faintly in a stray beam of moonlight.

Sindbad wanted to pick it up to see it better, and put out a finger, and the snail shrank into its shell. Talia tried to turn it to the left with a thin twig, but it went around the twig and returned to its path.

Kalila said, “This little one carries no map. But it feels the damp. And where there is much damp, there is a stream. Didn’t we pass a stream near the camp?”

Sindbad was silent for the first time in an hour. Then he said quietly, “And who says I wasn’t thinking of that?”

They walked behind the silver line, Talia holding Sindbad’s sleeve and he holding Kalila’s tail. The way was slow, Sindbad’s sandal sinking into the wet leaves, Talia counting her steps in a whisper, roots blocking them, but the ground grew colder and dewier. Then they heard a murmur. The trees opened, and on the far bank they saw the flame of the camp fire flickering.

Talia turned around. The snail had reached the water’s edge and stopped, its horns moving. She took from her pocket a lettuce leaf she had been saving for tomorrow, set it in front of the snail, and left it to eat in peace.

Activity

After rain, or after sprinkling water in a garden, look with a grown-up for the shining trail a snail leaves. Follow it with your eyes by the light of a small flashlight, and do not touch the animal.

Questions for the guide

  1. How did they know the snail was heading toward water?
  2. What would you do if you got lost with your friends in a place you did not know, and whom would you wait for to look for you?

44. The Mirror of the Sea

مِرْآةُ الْبَحْرِ

The fishermen call a small bay at the tip of the island “the Mirror of the Sea,” because at dawn its water goes still as a sheet of glass. Sindbad, Talia and Kalila reached it with the dew wetting their feet and the air full of the smell of salt and seaweed. The sun had not yet risen, and the gravel crunched softly under their sandals. Talia stood at the edge, listening, and Kalila sat on a rock licking her paw.

Sindbad bent over the water and said, “At last, a mirror worthy of me! Now the world will see this face without dust.” He straightened his turban, smoothed his hair, and raised his chin. Suddenly a fish leapt, and his face broke into trembling rings.

“Who gave her permission?” he said, and waved a hand as if shooing a fly. The water rippled more, and his face stretched and shrank like dough.

Kalila said, “Sit down. Water shows you nothing while you are stirring it.”

He sat, grumbling, on a wet stone, and felt the cold rise from the stone into his back, and began counting the remaining rings to escape his boredom. A long minute passed, and another. Then the bay grew so still that nothing could be heard but the plink of a drop falling from a rock.

Sindbad looked, and saw his face, and then saw behind his face things he had not noticed before: a pink cloud walking slowly, a gull circling high up, and Talia’s face smiling beside his own.

Talia said, “I see a shell on the bottom. It was hidden until the water settled.” From far off they heard the bell of a boat going out to fish, and a light mist rose from the water as if the bay were breathing.

Kalila said, “And I see a fish waiting to leap again. She won’t ask anyone’s permission.”

Sindbad looked on and on, and forgot to talk. Then his voice rose: “Look! A cloud in the shape of a donkey!”

He pointed at it with his finger, and forgot to smooth his hair.

Activity

Put a deep dish of water on a steady table, beside a grown-up. Stir the water with a finger, then wait until it is still, and look into it. Draw everything that appeared in the water except your own face.

Questions for the guide

  1. What changed in the water when Sindbad sat still?
  2. What do you think you would see if you looked into still water near your home?

45. A Book That Is Not Read with the Eyes

كِتَابٌ لَا يُقْرَأُ بِالْعَيْنِ

The cellar was cold, with a smell of old wood and dust. Ali went down its steps with a small lamp, with Sindbad, Talia and Kalila behind him, while his grandfather stood at the top of the stairs watching. The wooden steps creaked under their feet, and water dripped from somewhere far off like a slow clock.

In a forgotten chest Ali found a heavy book bound in rough leather. He opened it and saw no letters. He saw thick yellow pages with small dots on them. He turned it over twice and shook it, in case the letters would fall out.

Ali said, “I came down all these steps for blank paper!”

Sindbad said, “It’s ink that dried and fell off. I know about these things.”

Kalila said, “You know everything, Sindbad, except the answer.” Sindbad cleared his throat and said he had been joking.

Talia said nothing. She stretched out a fingertip and ran it slowly over the page, line after line. The paper was warm and strange to touch, and the dots tapped lightly against her skin.

She said, “The dots gather. Three here, and two there, and then the three come again. Like letters.”

The grandfather’s voice reached them from the top of the stairs: “That is the hand of my friend the carpenter. He was blind, and he read with his fingers faster than I read with my eyes. Read slowly, my daughter, and you will know.”

Ali handed the lamp to Sindbad with a care he did not know where he had got. And Talia read. She stumbled at first, and went over the line twice, while Sindbad held the lamp and, for the first time, held his tongue. Then she smiled.

“It says, ‘The key is under the blue jar.’”

Everyone turned to the shelf. There stood a blue jar covered in cobwebs. Ali lifted it with his strong arms, and beneath it a small copper key gleamed. The grandfather shook his head when he saw it, and said he had looked for it for twenty years, everywhere but here.

When they climbed back up, Sindbad was last, running a finger along the cellar wall and feeling what was in it.

Activity

Ask a grown-up for a sheet of thick paper and a pencil. Press the tip of the pencil on the paper to make raised dots, then turn the sheet over and run your finger over the other side. Describe to your companion what you feel, beginning with a sentence like: “Under my finger I find...”

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did Ali think the book was empty?
  2. What do you think your fingers can know that your eyes cannot?

46. The Mud That Swallows Footsteps

الْوَحْلُ الَّذِي يَبْتَلِعُ الْخُطَى

The ebb had left the shore a wide, shining salt-marsh, and the smell of mud and salt filled the place. Gulls cried above them, and the ground gleamed under the sun like the skin of a whale. On the bank an old fisherman sat mending his net, and beside him lay broad wooden planks.

Ali wanted to gather shells from the far side, so he took his basket and stepped onto the mud. “I’m strong. Nothing stops me.” After three steps his foot sank to the knee. He pulled it out with all his strength, and the other sank. The mud was cold and sticky, and made funny noises whenever he moved.

Sindbad ran toward him shouting, “Hold on! I’ll save you!” He grabbed Ali’s shoulder and pulled, and his own legs sank too. He cried, “This is the worst mud I have ever seen in my life!” Kalila said, “And how much mud have you seen?”

Kalila stood on firm ground and did not advance a step. “Stop pulling! Every pull takes you deeper.”

Ali cried, “But I’m stronger than this mud!”

“Mud doesn’t know how to wrestle. Relax your bodies, lean back, and spread your arms like wings.”

Sindbad looked at Ali, and Ali looked at Sindbad. It wasn’t easy for Ali to leave his legs without a pull, since he was used only to carrying and dragging. But he let out a long breath and leaned back. And after a little he felt that the mud was no longer pulling him.

Then the fisherman got up and pushed a broad plank over the mud until it reached them, and said in a calm voice, “Hold on to it and crawl like tortoises. I lost two sandals in this marsh myself when I was your age.”

They crawled out one after the other. Sindbad’s foot came out with a sound like a big kiss, and Ali’s foot came out without its sandal.

Ali stood on firm ground with one sandal and said, “The mud took my sandal as its fee.”

Then they walked home, Ali hopping on one foot and singing.

Activity

With a grown-up, mix two spoonfuls of cornflour (cornstarch) and a little water in a dish until it becomes a runny dough. Press it hard, then lower a finger into it slowly, and compare the two.

Questions for the guide

  1. What happened each time Ali pulled his foot?
  2. Has there been a moment when more speed and more force made things harder?

47. The Shadow That Vanished

الظِّلُّ الَّذِي اخْتَفَى

The midday heat in the desert grew so fierce that the sand stung feet through sandals. The caravan tent was close by, with the grown-ups drinking tea in its shade, and the smell of mint reached the children. The sun stood directly overhead, like a lamp hanging from a white ceiling.

Sindbad stopped suddenly and looked at the ground. “My shadow! Where is my shadow?”

He turned out his sleeves, looked under his turban, and turned around twice. He asked a standing camel, “Have you seen my shadow?” The camel chewed slowly and did not answer. Then he ran after the shadow of a rock and cried, “Is this it?” No. He ran after the shadow of a bird passing in the air, and called it by name: “Shadow, come back!” No one answered, except a stifled laugh from the direction of the tent.

Talia said, “Look at my shadow.” Hers was a small round patch right under her feet. Kalila’s was the same.

Sindbad panted, “Yours stayed, and mine ran away!”

Kalila said, “We don’t know yet.” And she wiped her whiskers with her paw. “Let’s watch.”

Talia drove a straight stick, an arm’s length tall, into the sand, and drew a line with her finger at the tip of its short shadow. Time passed slowly, and Sindbad poked at the ground with his toe and fussed. They sat on a mat under the tent drinking cold water, and every little while Talia went to the stick and drew a new line. The lines moved farther apart, bit by bit, and the stick’s shadow grew longer and leaned toward the east.

At mid-afternoon Sindbad stood in the yellow sun, and a long thin shadow stretched across the sand.

He cried, “It’s here! It’s back! And look at it, it’s twice as tall as I am!”

Kalila said, “It never ran away. It was under your sandals all noon, looking up at you while you ran.”

Sindbad put his foot on the head of the shadow and said, “Got you.”

Activity

Push a stick or a pencil into a lump of clay on sunny ground, and draw a line in chalk at the tip of its shadow. Come back after an hour and draw a second line, then compare the two lines with a grown-up. Do not look straight at the sun.

Questions for the guide

  1. Where was Sindbad’s shadow at noon, and what did Talia do to find out?
  2. Do you remember something you thought was lost and then found close by?

48. The Cardamom Tin

عُلْبَةُ الْهَيْلِ

A dust storm blew up suddenly on the road, and the sky turned yellow as tea. Sindbad, Talia and Kalila ran to the nearest house, an old empty stone house on the caravan road, and pushed open its rickety door and went in. The caravan guide had promised to come to them when the wind dropped.

The wind whistled outside, and the dust inside the house was thicker than outside. It floated in a weak beam of light coming through a broken window. The three sat with their backs to the wall, Sindbad covering his nose with the end of his turban and offering the other end to Talia. A little sneeze came from Kalila, which made everyone laugh.

On a high shelf Talia found a small tin box. She opened it carefully, and a familiar fragrance rose from it, cardamom and coffee. Inside were dried cardamom pods and an old folded paper.

Talia stood still. Then a tear ran down her cheek, then another, and the two tears were not from the dust.

Kalila noticed at once. She sat beside Talia and laid her chin on her knee and asked her nothing. And Sindbad, who had been about to say that he knew a finer house than this, shut his mouth.

Talia said in a husky voice, “My grandfather made coffee with this scent, and laughed. I remember his laugh well. But I can’t remember what he used to say to me.”

After a moment Sindbad said, “You remember his laugh? That is more than I remember of my grandfather. All I remember is that he dozed off in gatherings.”

Talia laughed as she wiped away her tears. Sindbad took out his little notebook and pencil and handed them to her. She wrote one long line, folded the page, and put it in the tin beside the old paper.

Outside the wind dropped, and they heard a camel bell coming closer, slowly, as if asking permission. Talia closed her hand on one cardamom pod, and smiled, with the smell of coffee filling the room.

Activity

Ask a grown-up for a clean empty tin, and put into it something whose smell you like, such as cardamom or orange peel. Smell it quietly, then tell your companion about a person or a place the smell reminds you of.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Kalila and Sindbad do when they saw Talia’s tears?
  2. If you see a sad friend, what can you quietly do?

49. The Compass in the Guest’s Pocket

الْبُوصُلَةُ فِي جَيْبِ الضَّيْفِ

In a small inn on the caravan road, a thin old man sat near the hearth turning something between his fingers. The wood crackled and the smell of lentils rose from the pot. The thing glinted in the firelight, and Sindbad froze where he stood. It was a brass compass with a cracked glass.

“That is my compass!” cried Sindbad, and everyone in the inn turned to him. The innkeeper went on wiping cups and pretended not to hear. “I lost it on the hills!”

The old man lifted his head slowly. His eyes were kind and wandering. “I don’t remember where it came from, my son. I forget a great deal. I forget people’s names, and I forget where I put my sandals.”

Sindbad turned the compass over gently. On its back was a little wave that he had scratched there himself with a nail one day. “This is mine, and this is my wave.”

The old man opened his hand at once. “Then take it.”

But Sindbad did not take it. He saw that the compass needle was trembling to no purpose, and that the old man’s hand trembled with it in just the same way. He sat down beside him and asked, “Why have you held on to it so long?”

After a while the old man said, “Because it points to a place. Even broken, it points to something. And I don’t want to forget that I have a home.”

Kalila fell asleep on the floor near the hearth, and Sindbad stayed for hours asking the old man about the smell of his home and the sound of the trees in his village. Piece by piece the old man remembered: two palms near the door, and a stream that sang in the morning. Sindbad wrote it all on a paper and gave it to the innkeeper to ask the caravans about a village with two palms and a singing stream.

At dawn the old man held the compass out to him again. Sindbad pushed it gently back into his hand and said, “Keep it with you. I know the way now with my own feet.”

The old man put the compass in his pocket, and patted it twice, and then patted it a third time to be sure.

Activity

Sit with an older person in your family and ask them to tell you about the smell of their old home, or a sound they used to hear there. Write what they say on a sheet of paper, and leave it with them.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did Sindbad not take his compass, although it was his?
  2. What can you do for a person who forgets many things?

50. The Three Things on the Last Morning

الْأَشْيَاءُ الثَّلَاثَةُ فِي الصَّبَاحِ الْأَخِيرِ

At a crossroads with an old olive tree, Sindbad woke before everyone. The sun was showing behind the hills as a thin orange ribbon, the air was cold and smelled of dew, and the sparrows were debating in the branches. He put his hand in his pocket and found no compass, no map, no breadcrumbs, only a sharp piece of glass that scratched his finger. He put in the other hand too, to be sure.

He sat on a rock. Talia came and sat. Ali came and sat. And Kalila sat, yawning. An olive dropped from the tree now and then and rolled over the gravel with a small sound.

Sindbad said, “I want to give you something great, worthy of a journey like this.” And he turned out his pocket. “But I have nothing. This is the first morning of my life that I have nothing to boast about.”

Ali laughed. He put in Sindbad’s palm a smooth stone that he had taken from the stream yesterday. “So that you have something heavy in your pocket that settles you.”

“A stone? That is what you give me on the journey of a lifetime?”

“If you don’t like it, give it back.” So Sindbad closed his fingers quickly over the stone.

Talia took from her sleeve a dry lemon seed and said, “Plant it somewhere you will stay a long time.”

Kalila held out her paw and said, “And this is a hair that fell from my whisker yesterday. So that you remember you have a friend who makes fun of you.” The three laughed, and Sindbad laughed longest.

He put the three things in his pocket, and Talia heard the stone tap the glass with a small knock. Then Sindbad looked at the two paths, the right and the left. He opened his mouth to say something grand, then closed it and said, “I have no compass. So what do we do?”

Talia listened, then said, “From the right comes the sound of sheep bells.” Ali said, “And sheep never stray far from water.” And Kalila lifted her nose and said, “Nor from bread, if my nose is right this morning.”

They rose together and took the right-hand path, Talia in front, listening, and Sindbad behind her with his hand in his pocket on the stone. Another olive fell behind them, and rolled until it came to rest by the empty rock.

Activity

With a grown-up, choose three small things from your home or the garden, such as a leaf, a smooth stone and a coloured thread. Put them where a friend or a brother will find them, without telling them what is inside.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Ali, Talia and Kalila put in Sindbad’s pocket?
  2. If you were in their place, what would you choose to give him?
