11. Talia and the Sea That Whispered

تَالِيَا وَالْبَحْرُ الَّذِي هَمَسَ

The ebb tide had gone far out, leaving shining lines on the wet sand like fingers. Talia sat on a warm rock with a basket of shells in her lap. She lifted each shell to her ear, then lowered it slowly.

A week before, she had come to this beach with her family, and she knew no one. The children were playing ball far off, and she watched them, gripping her basket with tired hands. Only the shells did not ask her where she had come from. Each one whispered in a different voice, one like wind in an orchard, another like a pot boiling in a house far away.

Sindbad appeared, thumping the sand with his stick and shouting, “I am the most famous sailor on the seven seas!” Kalila walked slowly behind him. She saw the basket, and she saw Talia’s face, and she said, “Good morning. Is there room on this rock for three?” Talia smiled and moved over a little.

Sindbad picked up a shell and pressed it to his ear. “I hear nothing. Your shell is broken.” Talia said softly, “Try putting down your stick. And closing your mouth, too.”

Sindbad sat. He fidgeted, scratched his nose, and sighed. A gull went by, crying, and some minutes passed. Then his eyes widened. “Sh... sh... It sounds as if it’s breathing!”

“That is the tide coming back.” Talia raised a finger. “And there is a smaller sound under it. Listen.” Sindbad listened, and heard the bubbles of foam popping on the sand, one after another. A cool breeze passed, carrying the smell of salt, and the sand under his feet shrank and spread with every wave.

Kalila said, “It’s the first time I have seen you hear something before you answer it.”

Sindbad did not reply. He had moved the shell to his other ear as well.

When Talia turned around, she found that the ball had stopped. The children of the beach had drawn close, and the smallest raised a hand and asked, “Do you have a shell for me?” Talia held out the whole basket.

Activity

Sit by an open window or in a quiet yard with a grown-up. Hold a paper and pen, and draw a small line for every sound you hear, near and far. After three minutes, compare your drawing with the drawing of the person who sat with you.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad hear in the shell once he sat still?
  2. If you saw a new child sitting alone, what could you say to them?

12. The Dog Who Would Not Eat the Bread

الْكَلْبُ الَّذِي لَمْ يَأْكُلِ الْخُبْزَ

Fog covered the whole meadow, and the grass was wetting Sindbad’s feet. He and Kalila walked among the stones until they reached a gap in an old stone wall. There sat a big grey dog, staring into the fog as if it were a door waiting to be opened.

Sindbad said, “What a fine dog! Come here, my friend, I have a piece of bread.” He held out his hand, and the dog sniffed the bread and did not take it, and went back to the fog. Sindbad tried to draw him gently away by the collar, but the dog stayed fixed like one of the stones of the wall.

“I am the most generous host in all the lands, and he refuses my generosity!” said Sindbad, annoyed.

Kalila laughed. “Perhaps he has work to do. Don’t ask why he won’t eat. Ask what he is waiting for.”

Sindbad sat on a wet stone and, for once, kept quiet. He heard water dripping from the branches, he heard the dog’s slow breathing, and he smelled cold grass and wet wool. Then a thin sound came from far away: tilin... tilin. A little bell.

The dog’s ears shot up. He gave one bark and bolted into the fog. They followed the sound of the bell, and behind a thorn bush they found a small lamb whose wool had caught in the thorns. The bell on its neck rang whenever it shivered, and its small eyes shone with fear.

Sindbad reached out and pulled the lamb hard. “No!” said Kalila. “The thorns hold tighter the harder you pull. Take the wool out tuft by tuft.” Sindbad did so, his fingers moving with a slowness he had never known, while the dog licked the lamb’s ear to calm it.

At last the wool came free, and the lamb leapt to the dog’s side. From far off came the voice of a shepherdess calling, and behind her the flock appeared like small clouds.

Sindbad went back to the gap and held the bread out to the dog a second time. The dog took it gently in his teeth and set it down in front of the lamb.

Activity

Ask a grown-up to hide a small bell or a key in another room and to shake it now and then. Follow the sound with your ears as you walk across level ground, until you find it. Swap roles, and see which hiding place was hardest.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the dog do with the bread at first, and what did Kalila notice?
  2. If you saw a friend refuse something you offered, what would you ask?

13. Kalila and the Tortoise Who Was Late

كَلِيلَةُ وَالسُّلَحْفَاةُ الَّتِي تَأَخَّرَتْ

“I am a thousand times faster than she is,” said Sindbad, watching a tortoise walk along the dusty road. The sun was high, and the stones burned the feet. The tortoise went on patiently, the shell on her back gleaming like the roof of a tiny house.

Kalila said, “Where do you think she is going?” Sindbad pointed to a shining pool at the far end of the plain. “There, of course. I’ll beat her to it and wait for her, reading a book.”

Sindbad ran. He tripped on a tree root and leapt over a rat’s hole, and he shouted to a bird perched on a stone, “I’m racing a tortoise and winning!” He passed a rock, a palm tree, a hill, and thirst grew in his throat. Sweat ran into his eyes and his shoes filled with dust. When he reached the pool he found it was a mirage, shimmering on dry sand. He sat down, dejected, his waterskin empty. He stared at the horizon, waiting, and heard his stomach growl with hunger, so he took one lone date from his pocket and ate it in three bites.

An hour later Kalila arrived, and behind her came the tortoise, walking on and never once looking at the pool. At every patch of shade she stopped. She sniffed the ground, turned around a little, and went on.

Sindbad said, “Why does she take such a crooked route?” “Follow her and you will find out,” said Kalila.

Sindbad followed slowly, his stride shrinking to the size of hers. At a thorn bush, the tortoise walked in under the branches. There the sand was dark and cool, and it smelled clean, like the first rain. Sindbad put out his hand and felt the damp.

He dug with his hands, and water appeared, gathering in a small hollow. The tortoise drank first, and then Sindbad and Kalila drank from their cupped hands.

Sindbad looked at the wide prints of his boots on the far road, and at the tiny prints of the tortoise beside his foot, and he laughed. He sat in the shade of the bush with his legs stretched out, and the tortoise’s head came out of her shell near his toes.

Activity

Walk in a garden or a schoolyard with a grown-up, as slowly as you can manage. Stop at every patch of shade and touch the ground with your hand. Afterwards, begin with a sentence such as: “In the slowness I saw...”

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did Sindbad’s speed not bring him to the water?
  2. When did you go slowly and see something you did not see while you were rushing?

14. The Palm Tree That Bore Fruit Late

النَّخْلَةُ الَّتِي تَأَخَّرَ ثَمَرُهَا

Ten palm trees stood in a hot valley, and each bore heavy clusters of ripe dates, except one. This one was short and slender, and its fronds were green and carried nothing. Red dates hung from her sisters while wasps hummed around them, and she stood among them like a new girl in the class.

The neighbours said to her owner, Uncle Masoud, “Cut it down and plant another.” Uncle Masoud would shake his head and say, “I water her every evening, and I don’t know what is wrong with her.”

Sindbad and Kalila arrived in the late afternoon and heard the story. Sindbad said, “I know the answer! I’ll dig around her and look at her roots right now.”

Kalila said, “Dig only at the edge, with your hands, and don’t hurt anything.” Sindbad knelt in the warm sand. Under the crust the soil was damp and clung to his fingers.

Sindbad brushed away the moist soil with his palms. Thin white threads appeared, fine as spider silk, stretching through the earth, and not one of them was long.

Uncle Masoud said, “I moved her from another field a year ago, when the flood ate away her old place.”

Sindbad stared at the white threads. “Then she wasn’t idle. She was rebuilding her roots from nothing!” He put the earth back tenderly and patted it with his palm.

Uncle Masoud mended her basin with a ring of clay, and Sindbad poured a bucket of water into it every evening for as long as he stayed in the valley. When he grew weary of waiting, Kalila said, “Have you ever heard of a tree apologising for being late?”

Months passed. One spring Sindbad came through the valley again and found, in the heart of the short palm, a swollen green sheath. Sparrows circled it joyfully. The sun split it, and out came a white cluster of blossom, giving off a sweet scent.

Sindbad stood on tiptoe and touched the cluster with the tip of one finger, the way you touch something when you are afraid of waking a child.

Activity

Plant a bean or a lentil in a clear cup with damp cotton wool and put it on the windowsill. Draw what you see in the cup every day, even if there is nothing in it. After a week, gather the drawings and turn through them with someone you love.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad find when he dug with his hands at the edge of the basin?
  2. When did you wait a long time for something without knowing that it was growing underground?

15. The Puppy Who Barked into the Well

الْجَرْوُ الَّذِي نَبَحَ فِي الْبِئْرِ

The puppy was small in body and big in voice. That morning he stood by a stone well in a dry village and barked, “Woof! Woof!” The air was hot, and the women were carrying empty jars to a spring far off, because the well, so they said, had been dry for a month.

From the well came a deep answer: “Woof! Woof!”

The puppy backed away, the hair standing up along his back. Then he barked harder, and the answer came back harder. Sindbad arrived, laughing. “Little one, that’s your echo! It’s you, down in the well!” But the puppy understood not a word.

Kalila said, “Not everyone who answers wants a fight. Try sitting with him.”

Sindbad held the puppy gently, at a safe distance from the edge, and sat down. The puppy fell quiet from tiredness, and the well fell quiet. In that stillness his ears lifted together. From far off, beneath the stones, came a delicate sound: tik... tik... tik. And under the smell of old mud the puppy caught another smell that made his nose tremble: the smell of water.

“What is that?” whispered Sindbad. “Water,” said Kalila. “Drops falling from a crack in the wall. The well isn’t empty, as the villagers thought.”

Sindbad ran to the village and came back with two men and a bucket on a long rope. One man lowered the bucket while the other kept hold of the rope, and Sindbad stood well back from the edge, keeping the puppy close. The bucket came up wet and dripping. The women stood by with their jars, staring in wonder, and one of them said, “Who would have thought a puppy would teach us where the water is?”

They poured the water into a bowl, and the puppy dipped in his tongue and wiped his whiskers with his paw.

Then he walked toward the well with small, hesitant steps, and barked once, softly: “Woof.” The well answered: “Woof.” The puppy tilted his head and sat down beside it, listening.

Activity

Go with a grown-up to a place that returns an echo, such as a long corridor or an empty hall. Call out loudly once, then in a whisper, and listen to what comes back to you. Try a short word and a long word.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the puppy think was in the well when he heard his bark come back to him?
  2. Have you ever thought that someone was angry with you, and then found it was not so?

Section 4 · What the Small Teach

An ant, a bridge and an old door, each holding a lesson smaller than your palm.

16. The Ant and the Grain That Was Heavier Than She

النَّمْلَةُ وَالْحَبَّةُ الَّتِي كَانَتْ أَثْقَلَ مِنْهَا

On an old mud wall Sindbad saw an ant dragging a grain of wheat bigger than her head. The wall leaned, and the sand on it was fine as flour. The noon heat shimmered over the clay, and the wall smelled of sun-warmed earth.

Sindbad said, “I don’t think, Kalila, that she will ever reach the top.”

Kalila sat down. “And I think she will. Let’s wait.”

The ant climbed straight up from the foot of the wall, and the sand slid beneath her, and she tumbled with the grain all the way to the ground. She took hold of the grain and climbed again, from the same side. And fell again. Sindbad heard the grains of sand pouring down like light rain.

Sindbad began to count: “One, two, three!” Then he stretched out a finger to help her, and the ant turned the grain away from him, as if to say, “This one is mine.”

“You are counting her falls,” said Kalila, “and she is counting her steps.”

Sindbad scratched his head. “I once tried to climb a hill with a heavy load, and I announced to everyone that I was the strongest man in the village, and then I complained about my back for three days.” The ant did not lift her head to him.

After the fourth fall the ant did not climb straight away. She stopped, moved her antennae, and went around a small pebble lying on the sand. Then she wound her way from pebble to pebble, as if hopping up a staircase that no one else could see.

Sindbad cried, “She has chosen another way!” and forgot all his predictions.

The ant reached a crack high in the wall and vanished into it. For a moment the wall was still, with no sound but the air. Then dozens of ants came out and lined up along the wall, and the grain passed from mouth to mouth until it disappeared into the dark.

Sindbad stayed with his finger still stretched out. Then he looked at Kalila and said softly, “Shall I admit now that I was wrong, or in a little while?”

Activity

Take a small bag of lentils or rice, and with a grown-up build a small hill from books and cushions in a room. Carry the grains to the top with a spoon, choosing a new route each time. Notice which route worked.

Questions for the guide

  1. How did the ant behave after she had fallen four times?
  2. If you had something heavy in front of you and wanted to move it, what would you try?

17. The Bridge That Met in the Middle

الْجِسْرُ الَّذِي الْتَقَى فِي الْمُنْتَصَفِ

The flood had broken the bridge between the two villages in the middle, and its two ends stood on the two banks like severed arms. The river had gone calm again, reaching no higher than a grown man’s knee, but no one wanted to risk crossing it.

Sindbad and Kalila arrived nearby. On the east bank they saw Ali carrying heavy wooden planks on his shoulders and shouting, “I’ll build from here to the middle, and I need nobody!” On the west bank Talia stood with women and men who were passing up shorter planks, and she laid her ear on each plank and tapped it with her finger before she allowed it to be pushed out. The smell of fresh wood and its golden sawdust filled the air, and the sound of saws echoed between the banks.

By sunset the two halves of the bridge had been pushed out from both sides, but between their ends a gap remained, two arm-lengths wide. The people stood looking at it in silence. Kalila laughed. “Ali measured with his big stride, and Talia measured with her small handspan!”

Ali was furious. He lifted the longest plank in the pile by himself and walked with it along the bridge toward the gap. Halfway there, the plank began to shake. Talia heard a thin sound: kreek. “Stop! The wood is groaning!”

Ali froze. All the weight was on his shoulder, and the bridge swayed under his feet. Kalila’s voice came, calm: “Why are you carrying all of it? The ends of the plank have hands waiting.”

Ali lowered one end of the plank and pushed the other toward the gap. From the west bank many hands came and took hold of that end. The plank settled over the gap with a muffled sound: thunk.

Ali looked at his light shoulder and saw his hands trembling, with no load in them for the first time. He said nothing. And the first to cross the bridge was a white nanny goat, who walked over without fear, as if passing through the door of her own house.

Activity

Ask a grown-up to help you carry something big, such as a shopping bag or a sheet of cardboard. Each of you stand at one end and walk together a short distance. Then swap places, and notice how your hands feel different.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did the two halves of the bridge not meet in the middle?
  2. What did Ali do when the plank shook, and what would you have done in his place?

18. The Whale That Ran Aground

الْحُوتُ الَّذِي جَنَحَ إِلَى الشَّاطِئِ

At dawn, before the sun rose, Sindbad saw something huge and black on the sand of the beach. He took it for a rock. Then a long breath came out of it, like the sound of a great bellows.

“A whale!” cried Sindbad, and he ran to it. “I’ll push it into the water myself!” He set his shoulder against the smooth skin and pushed with all his strength, and the whale did not move a hand’s breadth. He sat down, panting. The air was cold and smelled of fish and seaweed, and in the whale’s skin a great eye, as big as a hand, looked at him calmly, as if it did not understand what was happening.

Kalila came running. “Its skin will dry out in the sun, so let’s keep it wet. And remember: never cover the blowhole on top of its head.”

Sindbad fetched a bucket from the nearest house, filled it from the sea, and poured it over the whale’s back, calling the people at the top of his voice. The fishermen came out, and the women and children, with pots and pieces of cloth. They stood in two lines between the sea and the whale and passed buckets from hand to hand, the way people do when they put out a fire.

Hours passed. The sun climbed, the water splashed, and Sindbad was drenched to the knees. An old fisherman said, “The tide will rise toward sunset. We’ll dig him a short channel that will carry him to the water when it arrives.”

They dug with shovels and with their hands. At sunset the sea began to creep in. Its water reached the whale’s tail, then its belly. The huge body shivered and slid a hand’s breadth, then two. The women clapped, and the fishermen raised their lanterns.

The whale surged into deep water, and a shining spray rose from its back in the lantern light, and then it vanished into the dark. For a moment everyone heard the sea breathing.

Sindbad stood alone on the wet sand, still holding his bucket. He poured what was left in it on the empty place the whale had left, and the sand drank it dark.

Activity

Wet two small towels and hang one in the sun and the other in the shade, beside a grown-up. Every ten minutes touch each one and compare them. Then draw what happened to them.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did Sindbad’s pushing fail when he pushed the whale alone, and what did the people do together?
  2. When did you take part in a big job that no one could have done alone?

19. The Mulberry Tree in the Mosque Courtyard

شَجَرَةُ التُّوتِ فِي سَاحَةِ الْمَسْجِدِ

An old mulberry tree stood in the courtyard of a small mosque, its branches stretching out over the white tiles. The children loved it. They picked its berries and stained their fingers with them, then ran from the caretaker, laughing. In summer, black berries fell from it and made purple stains that never washed away, and in autumn its leaves blocked the drain.

Sindbad dragged his feet into the courtyard and found men gathered there. One of them said, “We will cut it down on Sunday. We’re tired of the stains and the leaves.” Sindbad backed him up. “Quite right, it’s a mess!”

Kalila gave him a long look and said nothing.

On Friday the heat grew so fierce that the tiles were like a frying pan. People came to the courtyard, and Sindbad saw children hopping from foot to foot and old women looking for the edge of a shadow. There was no shade anywhere except under the mulberry tree. A little girl, running toward it, said to her grandfather, “Will we cut the shade down on Sunday too?” He did not answer her.

People gathered beneath it. The air under its branches was several degrees cooler and smelled of green leaves. Sindbad remembered what he had said two days before, and felt his ears grow hot. He sat on its twisted trunk, and a mulberry fell on his sleeve and left a purple stain. Above him a sparrow turned over the leaves with its beak, and a bee hummed in the high branches.

Then the mosque caretaker stood up, a thin old man who sprinkled the tiles from a bucket every morning, and said, “We will not cut it down. We will trim the low branches and spread a net over the drain.”

Sindbad went up to him. “And the stains?” The caretaker laughed and handed him a broom. “The stains need a broom, not an axe.”

Sindbad swept the courtyard with the boys, and the caretaker gave each of them a handful of mulberries. When Sindbad looked at his sleeve, the purple stain had grown to the size of a coin, and he did not wipe it off.

Activity

Look for a big tree in your neighbourhood and sit under it with a grown-up on a sunny day. Feel with your hand how warm the ground is in the shade and outside it, and count the sounds you hear. Draw the tree and the people who sat beneath it.

Questions for the guide

  1. What happened in the mosque courtyard on Friday, and what did the people decide afterwards?
  2. Is there something in your home or school that tires us a little and helps us a great deal?

20. The Door No One Had Knocked On for a Year

الْبَابُ الَّذِي لَمْ يُقْرَعْ مُنْذُ سَنَةٍ

A large wooden door stood at the foot of a bare mountain, covered in cobwebs and a month’s dust on top of a month’s. The air was dry and stung the nose, and the gravel crunched under their feet. Sindbad stopped before it and said, “No one is here. This is a forgotten door.”

Kalila, sniffing the air, said, “I smell baked clay, and I see a thin thread of smoke above the mountain. This house is not empty.”

Sindbad struck the door with his fist: boom! boom! boom! No one answered. He struck harder and threw his shoulder against it, and it did not open. He sat down, rubbing his shoulder and muttering.

“Now, listen,” said Kalila. With one claw she gave a short knock, and waited, and gave a second, and waited. She knocked like someone counting breaths.

The silence held. Then, from behind the door, came the sound of slow steps and the grating of a key. The door opened a hand’s width, and the face of an old woman appeared, her hands smeared with red clay. On her wrists were bracelets of dried clay.

She said, “Welcome, both of you. I have been hard of hearing for years, and no one has knocked at my door for a year. But the water in my jar trembles at every quiet knock, and so I knew a guest was at the door.”

Sindbad went in and saw a long room filled with pots and jars and dishes, some finished, some still of damp clay. On the shelves, jars glazed turquoise shone like frozen water, and the smell of warm bread came from an oven in the corner. In the middle stood a jar full of water, with small circles spreading across its surface.

He asked, “And what about my first knocks?” The old woman smiled. “That was a commotion, and water doesn’t tremble at a commotion. It only splashes.”

She handed them a small ball of clay and said, “Make something before the tea goes cold.” Sindbad pressed his thumb into the warm clay and left a deep print that looked like no one else’s.

Activity

Pour water into a wide bowl and set it on a steady table beside a grown-up. Tap the edge of the table with one finger, using different strengths, and watch the surface of the water. Ask a friend to guess, without looking, which tap was the strongest.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did the door not open when Sindbad struck it hard, and what worked for Kalila?
  2. When did you try to call someone in a way that suited them, and not you?
