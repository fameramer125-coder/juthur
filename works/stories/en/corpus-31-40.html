A hand knocks twice, then waits.

31. The Stone at the Door

حَجَرُ الْبَابِ

The wind was pushing the cave’s wooden door, and it banged: knock, then stillness, then knock.

The travellers had settled for the night in a cave at the foot of the mountain. The air was cold, the smell of damp stone filled the nose, and the light of the small lamp trembled whenever the door knocked. Sindbad lay on his rug and grumbled, “I have crossed seven seas, and tonight a door defeats me!”

Ali stood up and braced the door with his shoulder. “I will hold it until morning.” But the wind did not tire, and his shoulder began to shake. Ali blocked the gap with two bags and a chest, and the wind shoved them aside as if they were straw, and Sindbad said from under his rug, “Nobody will sleep like this!” Kalila went on listening, her ears pricked.

Talia sat near the threshold and listened. The knocking had a rhythm: one, two, three. And between one knock and the next there was another sound, a small one, that rolled and scraped across the floor. She raised the lamp. At the foot of the door a smooth stone went back and forth with every gust, and in the floor of the threshold was a round hollow.

Kalila said, wiping her whiskers, “I heard the door all night. No one heard the stone.”

Talia asked nothing. She laid her palm on the cold floor to follow the small sound, and crawled until she was right under the door. The dirt beneath her cheek was cool and soft, and the stone rolled near her nose as if it walked on tiptoe.

Talia picked up the stone. It was warm as a hand. She turned it once and it would not go in, turned it another way and it settled into the hollow with a small click. The wind pushed the door, and its edge leaned on the stone and did not move.

Ali lifted his shoulder from the door and rubbed it with his hand. And everyone heard, for the first time, the sound of the wind passing by, away from the cave. Ali said in amazement, “A stone the size of an egg did what my shoulder couldn’t?” And Sindbad reached a hand out from under the rug and gave Ali half a date he had been saving for himself.

Activity

With a grown-up, listen to a door or a window in your home when the wind blows or when a hand moves it. Clap your hands to the rhythm of what you hear, then try to catch a small sound hiding beneath the big sound.

Questions for the guide

  1. What sound did Talia hear that neither Sindbad nor Ali heard?
  2. If you had been in the cave that night, what would you have done?

32. The Camel and the Thirsty Hollow

النَّاقَةُ وَالنُّقْرَةُ الْعَطْشَى

High in the village stood a red rock, with a hollow in its back that held two buckets of water and no more. The villagers drew from it little by little, the way the last bread is shared.

At noon one hot day a strange she-camel came, thin in the flank, her lips dry. She stretched out her long neck and drank, and drank, until the bottom of the hollow shone.

A man shouted, “She has taken all our water!” And Ali tightened a rope and said, “Shall I drive her off?”

Kalila did not answer. She was watching the camel, and the camel was watching no one. Having drunk, she was still sniffing the sand at the foot of the rock, and blowing on it softly.

Talia walked to where the camel sniffed. She leaned her cheek against the hot stone and listened. “I hear a drop... then another drop. In the crack, beneath the hollow.”

Everyone gathered around her. The sand at the foot of the rock was dark and damp, and from a thin crack water was seeping slowly, drop after drop, as it had for many years, and the sand drank it. No one had noticed, because they always climbed up to the hollow and never looked at the bottom of the rock.

“The camel saw it before we did,” said Kalila drily.

Ali said, “It trickles a little, and gets lost in the sand.” He put down his rope and fetched a small pick. He dug a narrow channel from the foot of the crack to a big wooden trough that had been forgotten behind the rock. The villagers came with their hands and baskets and lined the channel with clay so the sand could not drink the water. The sun scorched their backs, and no one complained. A little girl carried clay in her small hands, and came back for more.

By sunset the trough was half full. The camel knelt beside it and drank quietly, and a sheep stood by her, and a small girl with a jug. The man who had shouted first came and waited with his bucket until the camel was done, and then filled it without saying a word.

Activity

Put one full glass and two empty glasses on the table. Try with your hand how the water is shared out among the glasses, and then draw in your notebook a little channel that brings water to a thirsty place.

Questions for the guide

  1. What was the camel sniffing at the foot of the rock, and what did Talia find there?
  2. Do you know of something in your home or school where a little from everyone gathers until it is enough for all?

33. The Hollow Palm and the Offshoot

النَّخْلَةُ الْخَاوِيَةُ وَالْفَسِيلَةُ

“I’ll cut it down before sunset,” said Sindbad, holding the axe. “A palm with no fruit, hollow inside, its trunk cracked. I’ll use it for firewood and plant a new palm in its place.”

The palm stood at the edge of a small orchard, and the old woman who owned the orchard sat at its gate making rope from palm fibre. She said nothing, and did not lift her eyes from the fibre. The air held the smell of old dates and wet clay, and a bee hummed around the trunk.

Kalila said, “Before the axe, do you know what the bottom of it looks like?”

“A dry trunk,” said Sindbad.

“And the bottom of the bottom?”

Sindbad pursed his lips, but he knelt and brushed the soil aside with his hands. The soil was dry and brittle as a crust, and beneath it was other soil, damp and cool, that clung to his fingers. His nails filled with clay, the sweat ran into his eyes, and Kalila sat on the wall watching him without helping. And at the trunk, on the dark side, something small and green, the size of a finger, peeped out between the straw.

“An offshoot!” he cried.

Sindbad laid the axe aside. He moved the stone that was pressing on the offshoot, pulled out the grass that was choking it, and fetched a jar from the well, and when he lifted his head the sun had set and the axe lay where he had left it. He poured out the water little by little until the earth drank it. The old woman said, pulling her rope tight, “You watered today, and you will water tomorrow.”

Sindbad did not like patience, but he kept on watering. Every dawn he carried the jar and whispered to the offshoot, “I am the one who crossed the seas, so don’t be late for me.” Days upon days, and the offshoot hardly stirred, and the old woman laughed into her fibre without a sound. One day a hot wind blew, and he covered it with his cloak until evening.

Then one morning a frond unfurled, thin as a finger pointing at the sky. Sindbad measured it with his handspan, and wrote the number in charcoal on the trunk of the old palm.

Activity

Plant a seed or a date stone in a cup of soil near a window, and water it a little each day. Begin a small notebook with a sentence like “Today I saw in the cup...” and finish it with what you really see.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad find when he brushed the soil away?
  2. If you had something old that you wanted to throw out, what would you check first?

34. The Ant with the Broken Wing

النَّمْلَةُ ذَاتُ الْجَنَاحِ الْمَكْسُورِ

Sindbad had never known that ants have wings until he saw one dragging a severed wing across the gravel. The wing was clear as a thin leaf, and the ant dragged it and stumbled, and the sound of her tiny steps could be heard by anyone who listened. The gravel was hot in the sun, and the air smelled of dust.

“Poor thing!” said Sindbad. “I’ll rescue her.” And before anyone could ask, he picked her up between two fingers.

A moment later he dropped her, crying, “Ow! She bit me!” The ant ran away across the gravel and left the wing behind.

Kalila gave a short, dry laugh. “Rescued her from what?” Sindbad did not answer, and sucked his finger.

And Ali, adjusting the load on his back, said, “Leave her, Sindbad. We have a long road ahead.”

Talia stood at a distance, watching. She said softly, “Look. She isn’t running far.” The ant had circled and come back to the wing, and touched it with her antennae, as though asking it something.

Sindbad sat on the ground and fell silent, he who never fell silent. He laid his hand open on the dirt, beside the ant, and waited. The sun passed over the back of his hand. The ant did not climb on.

“She is in no hurry,” whispered Talia.

A beetle passed by him, and a fly landed on his shoulder, and he did not move. His back ached and his leg was going numb, while the ant circled her wing slowly as if measuring it. Kalila sat on a nearby rock and said nothing, this time.

Then the ant set off with the tip of the wing between her jaws, and climbed onto the edge of his hand without hesitation, and climbed down the other side, near a long column of ants carrying grains of wheat, each of which paused a moment to touch the one coming after her.

And she joined the line. The clear wing went swaying along above the column, like a small sail on a sea of gravel.

Sindbad looked at his finger, which had a tiny red dot on it. He smiled.

Activity

Go out to the garden or the balcony with a grown-up and sit still for five minutes near a line of ants. Do not touch them. Draw what you saw them carrying and where they were going.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the ant do after Sindbad dropped her?
  2. When did you feel like helping someone, and how did you know what they wanted?

35. The Shadow Sword

سَيْفُ الظِّلِّ

In the city market, at the stall of a long-moustached seller, Sindbad saw a wooden sword wrapped in red cloth. The seller said in a deep voice, “This is the Shadow Sword. It cuts shadows and hurts no one.” And he smiled a smile that Sindbad did not understand.

Sindbad bought it for three dirhams, telling himself that he had not haggled because the brave do not haggle. He carried it down the road the way one carries a crown, and would not let Ali touch it.

At night they stopped at an old inn, and Sindbad was given a small room at the end of the corridor. Kalila lit the lantern at the door and went to fetch water. The corridor creaked under footsteps and smelled of coffee smoke and old wood. Sindbad laid his sword beside his pillow like a knight in a war tent.

Sindbad raised his eyes. On the wall was a huge shadow with two crooked horns, swaying whenever the flame moved.

He froze. He heard his heart beating in his ears, and his throat went dry. Then he lifted the wooden sword and shouted, “I am Sindbad the Sailor!” and struck the shadow. Nothing happened. He struck again, and the sword hit the wall, and the shadow leaned and danced.

Kalila came back with the jug. She looked at the wall, then at the lantern, then at Sindbad, and said nothing. She picked up the lantern and turned it slowly until it stood in the far corner of the room.

The shadow shrank and shrivelled, and on a nail in the wall there appeared an old straw hat, its two brims bent up like horns.

Sindbad lowered the sword. He said softly, “I knew that.”

“Of course,” said Kalila, and sat down to pour water into two cups. Sindbad sat on the edge of the bed, his pride falling from his shoulders as dust falls.

He took the hat, hung it back on its nail, and propped the wooden sword against the wall beneath it. And the small shadow of the hat danced on the wall with every shiver of the flame.

Activity

Ask a grown-up to switch off the room light and leave on one flashlight. Make a big shadow with your hands on the wall, then bring the light close and move it away, and notice how the shadow changes.

Questions for the guide

  1. How did the shadow on the wall change when the lantern moved?
  2. Have you ever seen something that looked frightening and turned out to be something else?

A face, and behind it another face that we look at calmly.

36. The Mirror at the Bend in the Road

الْمِرْآةُ عِنْدَ مُنْعَطَفِ الطَّرِيقِ

At a narrow bend in the mountain road, a convex copper mirror had been hung on a post, so that a driver could see an oncoming cart before reaching the bend. The dust rose from the road, and the sun gleamed on the copper until it made the eyes close.

Sindbad said, quickening his pace, “A mirror that shows what’s behind us! A man who wants to go places does not look backward.” And he did not stop.

“One moment,” said Kalila, standing before the mirror. “Look once, and then go on however you like.”

Sindbad came back two steps, sighing. In the curved copper he saw the winding road they had covered that day, saw the little donkey standing and puffing, and everything in it was small and bent, as if the whole world were running inside a spoon. And he saw Ali, very far off, running and gasping, with a sack on his back. The sack was Sindbad’s sack. Behind him walked Talia, pulling the donkey, worn out with tiredness.

And from far off he heard Ali’s voice calling his name, faint as a breath, and he had not heard it before because his stride had been louder than every sound.

“When did you leave your sack?” asked Kalila.

Sindbad bowed his head, and his ears turned red. He had forgotten it at the spring, where he had drunk and filled his waterskin, and had walked alone all morning at a speed no one could keep up with. He had wanted to show everyone that he could outrun the sun, and he had not looked back once.

He said nothing. He sat on a stone near the post, took the waterskin from his belt, and waited. He heard the donkey’s tread on the gravel before he saw it.

And when Ali arrived, panting, and made to hand him the sack, Sindbad’s hand had got there first, holding the waterskin open.

Ali drank and wiped his forehead. Then Sindbad lifted his sack off the ground with both hands, and this time he walked beside the donkey.

Activity

Sit on the floor in a safe room and hold a small unbreakable mirror steady at shoulder height. Look into it to see who stands behind you or what moves there, and say aloud what you see.

Questions for the guide

  1. What appeared in the mirror that Sindbad could not see while he looked straight ahead?
  2. If you walked fast at school, who might be behind you?

37. Kalila and the Thunder

كَلِيلَةُ وَالرَّعْدُ

Nothing frightened Kalila. Once she had gone into a lion’s den looking for a shortcut, and laughed in a snake’s face. But when she heard the first thunder over the hills, she froze where she stood.

They were in another cave, wide and dry, high up the valley, with no door and no stone to roll. The wind blew with the smell of rain on dust, a white flash lit the cave wall, and then came the sound: a heavy boom like a stone rolled across the sky. Kalila’s ears quivered and she turned toward the corner, and tucked her tail under her belly.

Sindbad saw her bristling tail. He opened his mouth to say something funny and closed it again. He sat down beside her and spread the edge of his cloak until it touched her tail. The wool was warm from his body, and Ali and Talia slept at the entrance, knowing nothing.

“We had a burrow by the river,” said Kalila suddenly, very softly. “A thunder like this came, and then the water came. I was small. It was cold, and it had a voice like the thunder itself.” She had never said this to anyone.

Sindbad did not say that the cave was dry. He did not say that she was grown now. When the flash came again he said, “One, two, three.” The thunder boomed.

“What is that?” asked Kalila, lifting her head a little.

“Counting. A sailor told me that the number between the flash and the boom tells how far away the storm is.” The lightning came a third time: “One, two, three, four, five.” The thunder was softer.

The next time they counted together, he aloud and she softly: “Seven, eight, nine.” The storm was moving away from the hills.

Outside, the rain was tapping the rock lightly, as if someone were counting with them. And by the count of ten Kalila’s head had settled on the edge of his cloak, and her tail was soft as always.

Activity

If a thunderstorm comes, sit with a grown-up behind a closed window and count in a quiet voice the seconds between the flash and the boom. If no storm comes, count the ticks of a clock together up to ten, then try counting aloud while the other counts softly.

Questions for the guide

  1. What made Kalila afraid of thunder, and what did Sindbad do next?
  2. When a friend is afraid of something you think is ordinary, how can you sit beside them?

38. The Shepherd and the Lame Wolf

الرَّاعِي وَالذِّئْبُ الْأَعْرَجُ

The valley smelled of wool and smoke, and at its edge stood a pen of branches where a poor shepherd spent the night with his sheep. He had two dogs, a rusty lantern, and a loaf of bread that he divided each evening between himself and his big dog.

Sindbad stopped with him one night after losing his way. He sat by the fire eating and telling of seas without number, and the shepherd nodded whenever Sindbad mentioned a whale bigger than the last, and made no comment, until out of the dark came a long howl, and the fire crackled.

“A wolf!” whispered Sindbad.

The shepherd did not jump up in haste. He took his lantern and raised it on a staff until it lit the fence of the pen, and counted his sheep one by one, and then pulled the knot of the gate tight, twice. He said, “I tighten the knot, and then I trust in God.” He went back to the fire and began to hum an old tune, with a dog on either side of him and sparks flying from the wood.

The wind carried to them the smell of a wet animal, and they heard gravel rolling under a hesitant foot. At the edge of the light the wolf appeared. He was grey and thin, dragging his front paw, standing on three legs. The two dogs gave a single deep bark, and the wolf drew back two steps. The big dog was trembling with anger, and the shepherd laid a hand on his head, and he sat. The sheep were crowding behind the branches, hearing the humming and growing calm.

“Why didn’t he attack?” asked Sindbad, his voice shaking.

The shepherd said, “Look at his paw. There has been a thorn in it for days. He can’t run, and he can’t fight two dogs.” And he added, “And the fire and the lantern keep him away from the pen.”

The shepherd reached out and threw him, from very far off, a bone he had been saving. The wolf dragged it away and went into the dark, limping.

The shepherd broke his loaf in two and held half out to Sindbad and half to the dog, and Sindbad chewed slowly, and could not remember when dry bread had tasted so good.

Activity

Before sunset, sit with a grown-up at home and draw a lantern with a circle of light around it. Then add to the drawing whatever makes the night safe in your home, such as a lock, a lamp or someone close by.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the shepherd do to protect his flock, and what did he notice about the wolf’s paw?
  2. What in your home does the work of the lantern, the knot and the two dogs?

39. The Blue Scarf

الْوِشَاحُ الْأَزْرَقُ

A sand-laden wind blasted through the caravan market, scattering cloth from the stalls; tents groaned, and sand scoured the faces of the camels. Sindbad shut his eyes and shouted, “I can’t see a thing! This never happens at sea!”

“The sea has its troubles,” said Kalila. She opened his old wooden chest, which Sindbad had carried for years without opening, its lock rusty, and took out a folded piece of blue cloth.

“At least the sea washes my face!” shouted Sindbad, coughing, and he gripped his turban so it would not fly off.

“Take this. Wrap your face in it.”

Sindbad wrapped the cloth around his nose and mouth. It was soft as water, and its smell was a mixture of cardamom and old soap and warm bread, a smell he knew and could not place. He stood for one silent minute, the wind beating the cloth against his shoulders. In his head he saw a small kitchen filled with steam and the smoke of a clay oven, and two wrinkled hands kneading, and a voice calling his name as he ran toward the door. His eyes were wet, and he told himself it was the sand.

“It was my grandmother’s,” he said suddenly, in a muffled voice from behind the cloth. “She covered her head with it when she baked.” And Kalila looked toward the camels, to leave him his face.

“I know. A corner of it has been peeping out of the crack in the chest for a year. And now let’s shut it, because the sand is getting in.”

The wind dropped after an hour, and the traders came out from under the awnings to gather their goods, and everyone sat shaking the sand out of their clothes. Sindbad lowered the scarf from his face. On one end was a little star embroidered in white thread, which he had never seen before. He brushed the sand off and ran his thumb over the star and felt the tiny stitches, one after another. He folded it in four, tucked it into the pocket of his jacket, and then sat on the chest itself, having forgotten that he had been complaining.

Then he closed the chest gently, and brushed the sand off its lid with his sleeve.

Activity

With a grown-up, open a drawer or a box in your home and choose an old piece of cloth or something like it. Smell it, feel it, then ask someone who knows: whose was it? Begin its story with a sentence such as: “This piece used to be...”

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the smell of the scarf bring back to Sindbad’s memory?
  2. What old thing in your home has a smell or a feel that you love?

40. The Cracked Bell in the Harbour

الْجَرَسُ الْمَشْقُوقُ فِي الْمِينَاءِ

Sindbad stepped off the ship and stood on the quay a long time, the smell of tar and fish filling his nose and gulls crying above his head. Nothing was as he had left it. The old quarter had become a market of white stone, the shops had names he did not know, and people passed him without turning their heads.

“This city is not my city,” he said, and sat on a coil of rope, and put his head in his hands.

Kalila sat on the same rope and did not speak. After a few minutes she asked, “What was the first thing you used to hear when you woke up here?”

Sindbad thought, and raised his head. “The harbour bell. It was cracked. It rang with a hoarse ring, sometimes high and sometimes low, as if it were coughing.”

“Then listen.” She said it without looking at him, and shut her own eyes too.

Sindbad fell quiet. He held his breath until he heard his heartbeat, and then heard what lay beyond it. Through the din of the sellers and the creak of the winches, from far off came a hoarse ringing, rising and falling. He walked toward it, crossing the fish market with its smell clinging to his clothes, going around a new building and through a narrow lane, until he found it hanging from a wooden post at the end of the quay, rusted and gone green.

Beside the post sat an old woman selling hot bread, her hands white with flour and the clay oven glowing behind her. The bell rope was tied in a knot, and the clapper struck the edge of the crack, and out came that sound. She raised her eyes and said, “There used to be a boy here who raced the bell every morning, and said it was coughing from the cold. I think that was you, my son.” Sindbad’s ears turned red, for he had never been as good at anything as he was at talking, and now he could not find a word.

She put a hot loaf in his hands. And the bell rang again, hoarse and cracked, and a boy carrying a basket turned to look at it and smiled, and Sindbad waved to him with his free hand.

Activity

Go with a grown-up on a short walk around your home or school, and follow three sounds and two smells. Draw a small map of the route, and mark on it symbols for what you heard and smelled.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Kalila ask Sindbad, and how did he find the bell?
  2. What sound or smell do you know in your home even from far away?
