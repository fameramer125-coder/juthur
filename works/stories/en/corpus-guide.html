Guide for the Reader

دليل المُرشِد

English edition

This guide is for the parent and the teacher, and it is short on purpose. Take from it what you need and leave the rest.

Before the story

Read the story to yourself once before you read it to the child, so that you know where to pause and where to smile. Choose a quiet place and put away the phone and other screens. One story a day is enough. If you read before sleep, dim the light, and do not start an activity with a lot of movement. Prepare the simple things in advance, such as paper and pencils, a small stone or a piece of string, so that you do not have to break off the tale to look for something.

How to read aloud

Read more slowly than you think you need to. Give each character a voice that can be recognised: Sindbad is quicker and louder, Kalila calmer and drier. Do not overact. Pause for a moment at each new scene, and let the child look at the picture. With a reader of nine or older, take turns with the paragraphs. If the child stumbles on a word, say it again correctly in your own next sentence, without stopping them. If the child laughs where you did not expect it, do not scold; laughter is one way of listening. With a shy child, let them point at the picture instead of speaking, which is also taking part. Keep the drawings and papers the child makes, if they wish, and show them to no one without the child’s permission.

How to ask

Each story has just two questions. The first is about what happened and what the child noticed; the second is about what the child would have done, or what the story brings to mind. After a question, wait a full ten seconds, because thinking takes time. Do not correct the answer, do not sum up the lesson, and do not ask why they did something. If the child says I do not know, say all right, and move on to the activity or close the book.

How to run the activities safely

Make the activity play, not an examination. Any activity that involves walking or closing the eyes should be on level ground, with you beside the child. Keep it away from water and from heights, and never in the dark without an adult close by. Do not make a child speak, draw or close their eyes; if the answer is no, the answer is accepted. Do not judge a drawing as beautiful or not; ask what is in it. In a group, any child may simply watch. In a classroom, read the story at the start of the lesson, ask the first question, and leave the activity for home if time is short.

Some stories mention a dog, a well, a fire, a pole or a high place. Treat these as part of the tale only. Where an activity echoes them, do it with a safe stand-in, such as a toy, a drawing or a chair, and never with a real animal, a real flame or a real edge.

When to stop

Stop at the first sign of real distress: crying, confusion, withdrawing, or a wish to leave the room. Sit beside the child and ask whether they would like to go on or leave it. A story may remind a child of something that happened to them, and you do not have to push them to talk about it. Be there with them, and read a lighter story the next day.

The limits of this book

This book is not psychotherapy, and it is no substitute for a doctor or a specialist. If a child’s sadness or fear goes on, or their sleep, eating or play changes, consult a specialist who knows how to help children.

If a child says that someone has hurt them

Believe them, listen quietly, and do not blame them. Then tell a responsible adult you trust at once; a teacher tells the school counsellor or the school leadership. Say to the child plainly: if anyone hurts you, tell a grown-up you trust, and that is not tale-telling and not shameful.

In every case, five quiet minutes with a child are enough, and you should never compare one child with another.
