A note on this edition

This volume gives the fifty-three Sensory Stories in English. Each story keeps one pulse: Anchor, Transformation, with the silent question placed where the Arabic places it, at the end of the change, and Integration. In Part One each story is followed by the Mentor’s Box, which has seven parts: articulation, movement, relational warmth, a sensory alternative, the discovery marker, the safety note, and the author’s own English bridge, kept as written. Part Two is the child’s layer: the same fifty-three stories as literary text alone, with the scaffolding hidden. The Mentor’s Box is for adults only and is never read to the child.

Every activity is done on level ground with an adult close by. Any child may stop, sit or simply watch at any moment, and a child is never pressed to speak, to touch or to taste anything.

Mentor’s Introduction

“Do not describe to the child what he will feel. Let him feel it, then describe what happened.”

These stories are for ages 8 to 12.

In this series Kalila and Sindbad appear as a girl and her grandfather, a different pair from the fox and the sailor of Books One and Two.

What Is a Sensory Story?

A short story in which the body begins before the words. It contains something to hold, touch, or see, then a small change in the body, then a calm return to the place. It carries no sermon, and it never tells the child what he ought to feel.

How Do You Read Aloud?

Slowly, following your own breath, in a low, steady voice. Pause for a breath at every period. Do not act it out and do not exaggerate. Direct your eyes to the child, not the page, whenever you can.

Silence

After the change comes a question about the body, followed by a silence measured in seconds. Count the seconds silently, and do not hurry an answer. The child may not answer, and that is exactly what is intended.

Safety

Read the safety note before you begin. Keep the space clear and the floor not slippery. In anything that involves spinning, closing the eyes, standing on a sloped surface, pulling a rope, water, or food, ask the child’s family about any allergy or illness, and wash hands. The child may open his eyes, sit down, or stop whenever he wishes. No one is touched without their consent, and any child may simply watch.

When to Stop

Stop immediately if the child complains of dizziness, pain, or a headache, or seems afraid or tense, or says the stop word you agreed on together. The child can stop at any moment without being asked why.

Not a Treatment

These stories do not diagnose and do not treat. If you notice deep anxiety or repeated pain, the right step is to consult a qualified specialist and to inform the child’s family.

After Reading

When the silence ends, you may ask: What happened in your body? Then listen. Do not correct, do not explain, and do not praise one answer over another. Whatever the child says is right, because it is a report about his own body. And if he says nothing, a great deal has still happened.

The Mentor’s Box

It is for you alone. It holds a sound to end with, a movement to accompany the story, a warmth to show, an alternative for anyone who needs one, a sign to guide you to what the child discovered, a safety note, and a line in English that sums up the story. It is not read to the child and is not shown to him.

Before stories 49 to 53, perform the breath ritual: one hand on the chest, three calm breaths, inhale then exhale, with no breath holding and no strain.

1. Stretching the Thread

النصُّ العربيّ

مَدُّ الْخَيْطِ

Root m-d-d يَمُدُّ، الْمَدُّ، مَمْدُودٌ · Vessel An omniscient narrator, an outside voice describing the scene

Anchor

A white cotton thread lies on the table, and its two ends are tossed far from each other.

Transformation

The boy picks up the thread and stretches it between his palms. The thread pulls tight until it almost rings under his thumbs.

Then one end wraps around his middle finger, one wide loop, and the boy smiles and looks at it.

His grip loosens without anyone telling it to. The thread sinks into a small arc, and his hands come closer to each other, as if the thread had breathed.

Silent question 4 seconds how are your shoulders now?

Integration

The boy lays the thread on the table, and it keeps the shape of the stretch his hands left behind: slightly curved, with its ends closer than they were.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation A long, quiet mim, lasting one full exhale.

Movement Slowly stretch the thread between the palms, then release it step by step.

Relational warmth The loop around the finger is a scene to watch, not a mistake to correct. It is enough for the adult to stay close with a calm face.

Sensory alternative A silk thread or soft wool for anyone bothered by the roughness of cotton. For anyone who needs more movement, a short elastic band.

Discovery marker The child loosens their grip on their own after the first loop.

Safety note The loop always stays wide, and the thread is never wrapped around the neck or the wrist. If the tip of the finger turns white or very red, unwind the thread at once. The elastic band is never pulled toward the face.

English bridge A thread is pulled taut, then eased into a soft curve, and the hands drift a little closer as the grip softens.

2. An Axis That Turns

النصُّ العربيّ

مِحْوَرٌ يَدُورُ

Root d-w-r يَدُورُ، الدَّوَرَانُ، دَوْرَةٌ · Vessel The spine’s monologue, a non-human voice speaking to the child

Anchor

Two feet planted on a rug, and a torso beginning to turn around itself.

Transformation

I am your spine, and I carry your head when you turn. Your torso twists, and I twist with it, one vertebra at a time.

After the third turn the head sways a little like a branch in the wind, and the wall seems to turn around you.

Then you stop, and fix your eyes on one small point on the wall. Here I grow straight: I rise, vertebra above vertebra, until your head lifts and your feet sink into the rug.

Silent question 5 seconds what do you notice in your body after the turning?

Integration

You are standing, and I am straight in your back, like a tent pole after the storm.

In a beam of sunlight on the floor, light dust spins and slows more and more until it settles.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters د و ر, that is d, w, r, in a rounded sound that circles in the mouth and then quiets down.

Movement A slow turn around yourself, then a calm stop with the gaze fixed on one point.

Relational warmth A slight sway is an experience of balance, not a danger. Laughter is welcome, and a child who tires is asked gently and sits down.

Sensory alternative Turning while seated on a stable chair, or turning the head alone, slowly, for anyone whose balance is weak.

Discovery marker The child fixes their gaze on their own when stopping.

Safety note A space clear of furniture on a soft floor, an adult present and close, three slow turns at most, no turning near stairs, and the eyes stay open. Do not do the experiment with a child who complains of an ear problem or of frequent dizziness. If the head spins, the child sits down until it settles.

English bridge The spine turns ring by ring with the body, and when the turning stops, it is the spine that stands tall.

3. A Weight That Lightens

النصُّ العربيّ

ثِقَلٌ يَخِفُّ

Root th-q-l يَثْقُلُ، ثَقُلَ، ثِقَلٌ · Vessel A diary, first person, told after the event

Anchor

Sitting on the rug with eyes closed, one open palm waiting for a small cushion and then a smooth stone.

Transformation

Today I sat on the rug and closed my eyes, and a cushion was placed in my palm.

I tensed my whole arm to lift what was in my palm, as if it were a boulder. It flew up faster than I expected, and I almost laughed.

Then the stone was placed in my palm. I lifted it with a lighter hand than the first time, and it grew heavy on my palm and my hand sank a little.

I opened my eyes and saw a stone no bigger than an egg. My arm had guessed the weight before it knew it.

Silent question 4 seconds what is your palm doing now?

Integration

I lifted the cushion again, and what had felt heavy in my arm grew light. I set the stone and the cushion down on the rug, and let my hands lie on my knees.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ث ق ل, that is th, q, l, in a falling tone, like a weight sinking.

Movement Lifting two objects of different weights with eyes closed, and noticing the difference in effort.

Relational warmth The arm’s surprise is a reason to smile, not a mistake. The adult shares the child’s laughter.

Sensory alternative Two weights with a strong contrast for anyone who finds it hard to tell them apart. For anyone who needs more movement, a quick switch between the two palms.

Discovery marker The child adjusts their grip on their own on the second lift.

Safety note The child stays seated with the hands close to the lap. The stone is smooth with rounded edges, no larger than an egg and not very heavy, and it is never put in the mouth. The child opens their eyes whenever they wish.

English bridge With closed eyes, the arm guesses a weight before it feels one, and the stone corrects the guess.

4. A Spot of Light

النصُّ العربيّ

بُقْعَةُ ضَوْءٍ

Root ḍ-w-ʾ الضَّوْءُ، يُضِيءُ، مُضِيئَةٌ · Vessel A dialogue between the eye and the light, two non-human voices

Anchor

A faint spot of light walking along the wall, and two eyes following it.

Transformation

The Eye: I stare at the spot of light, and it slips away from me and slides off.

The Light: I walk across the whole wall, and you pull your gaze tight onto a single point.

The Eye: I blinked. My eyelid went down and came up. The spot passed through the edges of my sight and I did not grab it, and so I saw it.

Silent question 4 seconds what are your eyelids doing now?

Integration

The eye closes its eyelids and opens them again. The spot of light on the wall is near the window now.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ض و ء, that is ḍ, w and the glottal stop, with a light openness, like a window being opened.

Movement Following a spot of light that moves slowly, then blinking slowly and deliberately.

Relational warmth The light slipping away from the eye is a chance to slow down, not a failure.

Sensory alternative A very dim light for anyone who is bothered by brightness. For anyone who needs more movement, follow the light with a gesture of the hand.

Discovery marker The child blinks on their own before the spot slips away from their sight.

Safety note Always a steady light from a soft flashlight, with no flicker, no flashing and no laser pointer, and the beam is never aimed at the eyes. If the child complains of a headache or tiredness, the exercise stops, and the child’s family is consulted if the child is known to have light sensitivity or seizures linked to it.

English bridge The eye chases the light and loses it; one slow blink, and the light is simply there.

5. The Hiding Letter

النصُّ العربيّ

حَرْفٌ يَخْتَبِئُ

Root b-y-n يَبِينُ، بَيْنَ، بَيَانٌ · Vessel A participating narrator, second person, who accompanies the child during the experience

Anchor

A large-print page filled with tangled lines, and among them one hidden letter.

Transformation

The page is in front of you. You stare at it with all your strength, and the lines grow more tangled and the letter grows more hidden.

You exhale slowly and relax your forehead. Then you let your eyes drop to the white space around the lines, not to the lines themselves.

The letter appears from among them, and you did not chase it.

Silent question 4 seconds where were your eyes when the letter appeared?

Integration

You lift your gaze to the window and what lies beyond it. Distant things grow clear little by little: a branch, then a wall, then a cloud.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ب ي ن, that is b, y, n, in a light, open tone.

Movement Fixing the gaze on a blurred background, then relaxing the eyes with a slow exhale, then moving the focus to the empty space.

Relational warmth Eye strain is a sign of effort, and a smile melts the tension.

Sensory alternative A light background and a dark letter for anyone who finds it hard to tell them apart. For anyone who needs more movement, turning the page over and then pausing.

Discovery marker The child moves their gaze to the empty space on their own after the first exhale.

Safety note The duration is short, the lighting is good, no one looks at the sun through the window, and the experience stops if the child complains of tired eyes or a headache.

English bridge Stare hard and the letter hides; let the gaze soften toward the white between the lines, and the shape steps out.

6. The Warmth of a Word

النصُّ العربيّ

دِفْءُ الْكَلِمَةِ

Root d-f-ʾ دِفْءٌ، دَافِئٌ، تَدْفِئَةٌ · Vessel A timed sensory experience, second person

Anchor

A warm stone in the right palm, and a cold stone from the shade in the left palm. The eyes are closed at the start.

Transformation

You close your eyes. In your right hand is a warm stone, and in your left is a cold stone. Where does the warmth live?

You open your eyes, and read on a card the word “دِفْءٍ”, warmth, as you touch the stone. Then you read the word “بُرُودَةٍ”, coldness, as you touch the cold stone.

Then the palms swap what is in them, and you read the two words a second time.

Silent question 5 seconds what do you notice in your palms now?

Integration

You set the two stones on the table and rub your palms together. The difference between them melts little by little.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters د ف ء, that is d, f and the glottal stop, in a warm, open tone.

Movement Holding the warm and the cold with eyes closed, then reading the word after the touch.

Relational warmth Frowning at the cold and smiling at the warmth is part of the discovery.

Sensory alternative Cold water and warm, not hot, water in two tightly sealed bags for anyone who is sensitive to the stone. For anyone who needs more movement, a quick swap between the palms.

Discovery marker The child reads the word without being asked after the second touch.

Safety note There is no ice in this experiment; the cold stone comes from the shade, not from the freezer, and it stays in the palm no more than half a minute. The stone is clean and as warm as the body, the adult tries it first on their wrist, and it is never hot. Hands are washed before and after the experiment. There is no food in this experiment, the child opens their eyes whenever they wish, and parents are asked about skin sensitivity to cold or fingers turning white or blue in the cold.

English bridge One palm holds warmth and the other holds cold, and a word is read between them, then read again after the hands swap.

7. The Pencil’s Conversation

النصُّ العربيّ

حِوَارُ الْقَلَمِ

Root q-b-ḍ أَقْبِضُ، قَبْضَةٌ، مَقْبِضٌ · Vessel A dialogue between the hand and the pencil, two non-human voices

Anchor

A pencil between the fingers, and the paper white and waiting.

Transformation

The Hand: I grip the pencil with all my strength.

The Pencil: I hear your pressure in my whole body, and I freeze. The line comes out trembling.

The Hand: I loosen my grip a little.

The Pencil: Now I can slide.

The hand listens, and the pencil flows across the paper, and the line grows smoother and longer.

Silent question 4 seconds how are your fingers holding the pencil now?

Integration

On the paper is one long line in which the pencil never lifted its head, and beside it are open fingers.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ق ب ض, that is q, b, ḍ, in a balanced tone that tightens and then relaxes.

Movement Gripping the pencil with exaggerated force, then gradually easing the grip.

Relational warmth The pencil’s protest is a sound game that the adult and the child share.

Sensory alternative A thick wax crayon for anyone who dislikes the feel of pencil. For anyone who needs more movement, big quick lines.

Discovery marker The child adjusts their pressure on their own after the pencil’s protest.

Safety note A pencil with a blunt tip, never pointed toward the face or the eyes, never put in the mouth, and the same goes for the wax crayon.

English bridge The fingers squeeze, the pencil goes stiff; the fingers let go, and the line runs on like water.

8. The Dance of the Step

النصُّ العربيّ

رَقْصَةُ الْخُطْوَةِ

Root m-sh-y مَشَيْتُ، يَمْشِي، الْمَشْيُ · Vessel A diary, first person, told after the event

Anchor

Heavy steps on the hall floor, and the sound of pounding filling the place.

Transformation

This morning I walked through the hall heavily. My heels struck the floor, and the sound filled the place like a drum. I laughed at the sound of them.

Then I walked slowly, and moved my weight from the heel to the front of the foot, the way water is passed between two palms.

The pounding vanished. And I heard another sound: the rustle of a sock on wood.

Silent question 4 seconds how does your foot touch the floor now?

Integration

I stopped. Under my socks were the planks of wood, and none of them moved.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters م ش ي, that is m, sh, y, in a light flow, gliding and not pounding.

Movement Slow walking, moving the weight from the heel to the front of the foot.

Relational warmth Heavy walking is an invitation to play, and the heavy pounding is part of the story.

Sensory alternative Walking on a sponge mat for anyone whose balance is weak. For anyone who needs more movement, walking to the beat of a quiet song.

Discovery marker The child shifts their weight on their own after the first heavy steps.

Safety note A dry, clean, non-slippery floor, bare feet or non-slip socks, and a path clear of obstacles.

English bridge The heel lifts, the weight rolls forward, and the loud footstep turns into a glide.

9. The Edge of Distance

النصُّ العربيّ

حَافَّةُ الْمَسَافَةِ

Root l-m-s يَلْمِسُ، اللَّمْسُ، مَلْمَسٌ · Vessel An omniscient narrator, a sensory scene

Anchor

An index finger passes over the surface of a low wooden table, and the wood is smooth beneath it.

Transformation

The boy touches the tabletop with the tip of his index finger and moves it slowly. The wood is smooth, then something changes: a thin line, then nothing.

The finger slips into the air, and his lips part.

He brings the finger back to the start of the path and passes over it a second time. This time he stops before the line by the width of a fingernail. The skin of his finger tightens, then relaxes.

Silent question 4 seconds what do you feel in your fingertip?

Integration

The boy lifts his hand and looks at the edge. Then he lays his whole palm flat on the wood, all the way to the rim, and finds that the edge holds the weight of a whole palm.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ل م س, that is l, m, s, as a light whisper that the air passes through.

Movement Running the finger over the surface up to the edge, stopping before the line, and noticing the skin tighten and then relax.

Relational warmth The finger slipping is not a mistake, but knowledge of limits.

Sensory alternative A surface covered with soft cloth for anyone who dislikes the feel of wood. For anyone who needs more movement, a quick pass and then a sudden stop.

Discovery marker The child stops on their own before the edge.

Safety note A low table whose edges are rounded or covered with soft tape, the child seated, and the experiment done with the hand alone, with no climbing and no leaning over the edge.

English bridge A fingertip slides over smooth wood, stops just short of the edge, and then a whole palm comes to rest there.

10. Rocking the Axis

النصُّ العربيّ

تَرْجِيحُ الْمِحْوَرِ

Root ʿ-d-l يَعْتَدِلُ، التَّعْدِيلُ، اعْتِدَالٌ · Vessel A dialogue between the board and the ground, two non-human voices

Anchor

A short wooden board tilting slightly on a mat, and two bare feet standing on it.

Transformation

The Board: I tilt under his feet, and the boy wobbles.

The Ground: And I am under all of you, and I do not tilt.

The boy’s knees bend a little. His weight moves from one foot to the other.

The Board: Whenever I tilt, his knees tilt with me.

The Ground: And I keep a place for his feet. The board levels.

Silent question 5 seconds how are your legs now?

Integration

The boy steps off the board onto the floor. The board rocks a little on its own, then rests, level.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ع د ل, that is ʿayn, d, l, in a balanced tone that leans and then levels.

Movement Standing on a slightly tilted surface, and shifting the weight slowly.

Relational warmth Swaying is the language of balance, not weakness. The adult stays within an arm’s reach of the child, and does not touch the child; the child may lean on the wall or the chair when needed.

Sensory alternative A nearly stable board, or a folded towel under one side. For anyone who needs more movement, a quick tilt and then stillness.

Discovery marker The child shifts their weight on their own.

Safety note A board that rises only a few centimeters off the ground, on a mat or soft floor, bare feet, a wall or sturdy chair within reach of the hand, and the child steps down whenever they wish. Anyone who feels dizzy sits down.

English bridge The board tilts, the knees soften, the weight travels, and the board comes level.

11. A Steady Base

النصُّ العربيّ

ثُبُوتُ الْقَاعِدَةِ

Root th-b-t ثَبَتَتْ، ثُبُوتٌ، ثَابِتٌ · Vessel A diary, first person, told after the event

Anchor

One foot trembling near the wall, and the other raised.

Transformation

Tonight I stood on one leg near the wall. My foot shook like a leaf, I swayed, and my eyes went wide.

I set the second foot down far from the first, a shoulder width apart or wider. My pelvis sank a little, my thighs relaxed, and my feet held as if they had roots. I became wide like a tree.

Then I closed my eyes. The weight spread across my feet, and nothing shook me.

Silent question 4 seconds what do you feel in your feet now?

Integration

I opened my eyes. My shadow on the wall is wide and still, and its head sits above lowered shoulders.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ث ب ت, that is th, b, t, in a falling tone, like a weight settling.

Movement Moving from a narrow stance on one leg to a wide stance on both legs.

Relational warmth Wobbling is not failure, but exploration of the base.

Sensory alternative A chair touched with the fingertips for anyone whose balance is weak. For anyone who needs more movement, a slow switch between the narrow and the wide stance.

Discovery marker The child widens their stance on their own.

Safety note A wall or sturdy chair within reach of the hand, a non-slippery floor, and an adult nearby. Standing on one leg only for a few seconds. The eyes are closed only in the wide stance, and are opened at the first sway.

English bridge On one leg the body trembles; planted wide like a tree, it settles into its bones.

12. Cold and Warmth

النصُّ العربيّ

بُرُودَةٌ وَدِفْءٌ

Root b-r-d بَرْدٌ، بَارِدٌ، بَارِدَةُ · Vessel A dialogue between the two palms

Anchor

A glass cup with cold water in it, and a piece of warm cloth. You hold the cup in your right hand and the cloth in your left. The right hand whispers, “Cold!” and the left answers, “Warm!”

Transformation

The fingers of your right hand tighten around the cup, then loosen a little. Where does the cold that began sharp as a pin spread? You set the cup on the table and bring your palms together, one against the other. Where does the warmth live?

Silent question 5 seconds what do you find between the cold and the warmth in your palms?

Integration

On the wall of the cup are small drops of dew, and in one of them is the mark of your finger. A slow drop runs from it toward the table.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation A whispered ب “b”, ر “r” and د “d”, starting sharp and then softening with the warmth.

Movement Hold a cup of cold water in one hand and a warm cloth in the other, then bring the palms together. The water is cold, not icy, and the cloth is warm, not hot.

Relational warmth The child is not asked to endure. If the child pulls a hand away, pulling away is an acceptable answer, and the experience is repeated after a moment.

Sensory alternative Lukewarm water instead of cold, or one palm only, and wipe the table if the dew wets it.

Discovery marker The child keeps a hand on the cold cup without pulling it away, or points to where the line between the cold and the warmth is.

Safety note The mentor tests the temperature of the water and the cloth by hand before the activity, and uses an unbreakable cup, or thick glass set on a napkin over a stable table, and the cloth is warmed only in warm water.

English bridge The cold starts as a pin, then spreads into something wide and slow, and the warm hand waits on the other side.

13. Stopping the Run

النصُّ العربيّ

تَوَقُّفُ الرَّكْضِ

Root r-k-d تَرْكُضُ، الرَّكْضِ، رَكْضٌ · Vessel A live action scene

Anchor

You run on dry grass, and your shoes tap the ground lightly. At the willow tree you want to stop, but your feet keep running two steps past the spot! Your eyes widen.

Transformation

You go back to the start and run again, a little slower. As you near the tree your knees bend, and your weight sinks down to the ground, and the ground takes it. You stop exactly at the spot. And the jolt that used to climb to your teeth does not climb. You put your palms on your knees.

Silent question 4 seconds where did the speed of the run go that was in your feet?

Integration

Where does the warmth live in your legs? Behind you in the grass is a line of footsteps, ending at the willow tree.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation The letters ر “r”, ك “k” and ض “d”, starting sharp and then softening, and ending in a long exhale.

Movement A light run, then a stop, then bending the knees, in an open, dry space free of obstacles, with the stopping point a line on the grass several meters before the willow, not the tree itself, with the shoes tied tight, and the mentor stands at the finish line, not in the child’s path.

Relational warmth Going past the spot is not a mistake, it is the first thing the body learns about absorbing movement.

Sensory alternative A foam mat indoors, or a quick walk instead of running for a child who tires or whose joint hurts.

Discovery marker The child bends their knees on their own the next time, or shortens the stride before the spot.

Safety note Check the grass before the session for holes, stones and dampness, and do not run on wet ground. Keep the distance short. The child stops at once if they feel dizziness or pain in the chest or a knee, and drinks water after running.

English bridge The line comes too fast, then the knees soften, the weight settles, and the grass keeps every footprint.

14. Arm’s Length

النصُّ العربيّ

طُولُ الذِّرَاعِ

Root t-w-l طَوِيلَةٌ، طُولِي، طُولُ · Vessel A non-human voice, the arm speaks

Anchor

You stand in a spacious corner of the room and lift your right arm in front of you in the air. The arm whispers, “How long am I? Where does my place end?”

Transformation

48. The Throat’s Secret

النصُّ العربيّ

سِرُّ الْحَلْقِ

Anchor

One hand on the throat, with light fingers that do not press. One word is said aloud, then whispered.

Transformation

I put my fingers on my throat and say aloud: “Water.” My fingers buzz as if a bee had landed on them.

My little brother says: “Now with the secret!”

I whisper: “Water.” The buzzing goes away. Nothing is left under my fingers but stillness, and a small warm breeze comes out of my mouth.

My brother puts his fingers on his throat and tries, then laughs: “It vanished! This is a secret only someone who has put a hand there can know.”

Silent question 4 seconds what do you feel under your fingers now?

Integration

We switch between the loud voice and the whisper three times with a new word, and our fingers know the difference before our ears do.

The last word we whisper, then we lower our hands to our knees and fall silent. Our throats stay still, and a warm, calm breath comes out of us.

Mentor’s Box adults only

Articulation Ha, «ه», is breath without voice, Seen, «س», is a whispered whistle, and Meem, «م», is voiced: it buzzes under the fingers when spoken aloud and falls silent when whispered.

Movement A hand on the throat without pressure, and a word said aloud, then the same word whispered.

Relational warmth If the child stumbles or laughs, the mentor shares a funny whisper with them.

Sensory alternative Whispering into a paper tube, or a hand on the chest instead of the throat for anyone who does not like their neck touched.

English bridge Spoken, the word hums beneath your fingertips. Whispered, it leaves only a small warm breath.

50. The Push of Steadiness

Part Two · The Child’s Layer · The Literary Text Alone

طَبَقَةُ الطِّفْلِ، النَّصُّ الأَدَبِيُّ فَقَطْ

Fifty-three stories for reading: the same texts with the scaffolding hidden, as the Arabic edition gives them for the child’s hands. No boxes and no questions: only the literature.

1. Stretching the Thread

مَدُّ الْخَيْطِ

A white cotton thread lies on the table, and its two ends are tossed far from each other.

The boy picks up the thread and stretches it between his palms. The thread pulls tight until it almost rings under his thumbs.

Then one end wraps around his middle finger, one wide loop, and the boy smiles and looks at it.

His grip loosens without anyone telling it to. The thread sinks into a small arc, and his hands come closer to each other, as if the thread had breathed.

The boy lays the thread on the table, and it keeps the shape of the stretch his hands left behind: slightly curved, with its ends closer than they were.

2. An Axis That Turns

مِحْوَرٌ يَدُورُ

Two feet planted on a rug, and a torso beginning to turn around itself.

I am your spine, and I carry your head when you turn. Your torso twists, and I twist with it, one vertebra at a time.

After the third turn the head sways a little like a branch in the wind, and the wall seems to turn around you.

Then you stop, and fix your eyes on one small point on the wall. Here I grow straight: I rise, vertebra above vertebra, until your head lifts and your feet sink into the rug.

You are standing, and I am straight in your back, like a tent pole after the storm.

In a beam of sunlight on the floor, light dust spins and slows more and more until it settles.

3. A Weight That Lightens

ثِقَلٌ يَخِفُّ

Sitting on the rug with eyes closed, one open palm waiting for a small cushion and then a smooth stone.

Today I sat on the rug and closed my eyes, and a cushion was placed in my palm.

I tensed my whole arm to lift what was in my palm, as if it were a boulder. It flew up faster than I expected, and I almost laughed.

Then the stone was placed in my palm. I lifted it with a lighter hand than the first time, and it grew heavy on my palm and my hand sank a little.

I opened my eyes and saw a stone no bigger than an egg. My arm had guessed the weight before it knew it.

I lifted the cushion again, and what had felt heavy in my arm grew light. I set the stone and the cushion down on the rug, and let my hands lie on my knees.

4. A Spot of Light

بُقْعَةُ ضَوْءٍ

A faint spot of light walking along the wall, and two eyes following it.

The Eye: I stare at the spot of light, and it slips away from me and slides off.

The Light: I walk across the whole wall, and you pull your gaze tight onto a single point.

The Eye: I blinked. My eyelid went down and came up. The spot passed through the edges of my sight and I did not grab it, and so I saw it.

The eye closes its eyelids and opens them again. The spot of light on the wall is near the window now.

5. The Hiding Letter

حَرْفٌ يَخْتَبِئُ

A large-print page filled with tangled lines, and among them one hidden letter.

The page is in front of you. You stare at it with all your strength, and the lines grow more tangled and the letter grows more hidden.

You exhale slowly and relax your forehead. Then you let your eyes drop to the white space around the lines, not to the lines themselves.

The letter appears from among them, and you did not chase it.

You lift your gaze to the window and what lies beyond it. Distant things grow clear little by little: a branch, then a wall, then a cloud.

6. The Warmth of a Word

دِفْءُ الْكَلِمَةِ

A warm stone in the right palm, and a cold stone from the shade in the left palm. The eyes are closed at the start.

You close your eyes. In your right hand is a warm stone, and in your left is a cold stone. Where does the warmth live?

You open your eyes, and read on a card the word “دِفْءٍ”, warmth, as you touch the stone. Then you read the word “بُرُودَةٍ”, coldness, as you touch the cold stone.

Then the palms swap what is in them, and you read the two words a second time.

You set the two stones on the table and rub your palms together. The difference between them melts little by little.

7. The Pencil’s Conversation

حِوَارُ الْقَلَمِ

A pencil between the fingers, and the paper white and waiting.

The Hand: I grip the pencil with all my strength.

The Pencil: I hear your pressure in my whole body, and I freeze. The line comes out trembling.

The Hand: I loosen my grip a little.

The Pencil: Now I can slide.

The hand listens, and the pencil flows across the paper, and the line grows smoother and longer.

On the paper is one long line in which the pencil never lifted its head, and beside it are open fingers.

8. The Dance of the Step

رَقْصَةُ الْخُطْوَةِ

Heavy steps on the hall floor, and the sound of pounding filling the place.

This morning I walked through the hall heavily. My heels struck the floor, and the sound filled the place like a drum. I laughed at the sound of them.

Then I walked slowly, and moved my weight from the heel to the front of the foot, the way water is passed between two palms.

The pounding vanished. And I heard another sound: the rustle of a sock on wood.

I stopped. Under my socks were the planks of wood, and none of them moved.

9. The Edge of Distance

حَافَّةُ الْمَسَافَةِ

An index finger passes over the surface of a low wooden table, and the wood is smooth beneath it.

The boy touches the tabletop with the tip of his index finger and moves it slowly. The wood is smooth, then something changes: a thin line, then nothing.

The finger slips into the air, and his lips part.

He brings the finger back to the start of the path and passes over it a second time. This time he stops before the line by the width of a fingernail. The skin of his finger tightens, then relaxes.

The boy lifts his hand and looks at the edge. Then he lays his whole palm flat on the wood, all the way to the rim, and finds that the edge holds the weight of a whole palm.

10. Rocking the Axis

تَرْجِيحُ الْمِحْوَرِ

A short wooden board tilting slightly on a mat, and two bare feet standing on it.

The Board: I tilt under his feet, and the boy wobbles.

The Ground: And I am under all of you, and I do not tilt.

The boy’s knees bend a little. His weight moves from one foot to the other.

The Board: Whenever I tilt, his knees tilt with me.

The Ground: And I keep a place for his feet. The board levels.

The boy steps off the board onto the floor. The board rocks a little on its own, then rests, level.

11. A Steady Base

ثُبُوتُ الْقَاعِدَةِ

One foot trembling near the wall, and the other raised.

Tonight I stood on one leg near the wall. My foot shook like a leaf, I swayed, and my eyes went wide.

I set the second foot down far from the first, a shoulder width apart or wider. My pelvis sank a little, my thighs relaxed, and my feet held as if they had roots. I became wide like a tree.

Then I closed my eyes. The weight spread across my feet, and nothing shook me.

I opened my eyes. My shadow on the wall is wide and still, and its head sits above lowered shoulders.

12. Cold and Warmth

بُرُودَةٌ وَدِفْءٌ

A glass cup with cold water in it, and a piece of warm cloth. You hold the cup in your right hand and the cloth in your left. The right hand whispers, “Cold!” and the left answers, “Warm!”

The fingers of your right hand tighten around the cup, then loosen a little. Where does the cold that began sharp as a pin spread? You set the cup on the table and bring your palms together, one against the other. Where does the warmth live?

On the wall of the cup are small drops of dew, and in one of them is the mark of your finger. A slow drop runs from it toward the table.

13. Stopping the Run

تَوَقُّفُ الرَّكْضِ

You run on dry grass, and your shoes tap the ground lightly. At the willow tree you want to stop, but your feet keep running two steps past the spot! Your eyes widen.

You go back to the start and run again, a little slower. As you near the tree your knees bend, and your weight sinks down to the ground, and the ground takes it. You stop exactly at the spot. And the jolt that used to climb to your teeth does not climb. You put your palms on your knees.

Where does the warmth live in your legs? Behind you in the grass is a line of footsteps, ending at the willow tree.

14. Arm’s Length

طُولُ الذِّرَاعِ

You stand in a spacious corner of the room and lift your right arm in front of you in the air. The arm whispers, “How long am I? Where does my place end?”

The arm stretches farther, and your fingers try to touch something that is not there. Then a gentle tremor appears in the muscle. The arm says, “Here. This is my length.” And it comes back by the width of a finger, and the tremor calms.

You lower your arm to your side and shake it gently, like a branch after the rain. On the floor is the shadow of a long arm, which gets shorter and then returns to the side of your shadow.

15. An Orderly Fall

سُقُوطٌ مُرَتَّبٌ

You and I sit on the floor, and behind us are big cushions stacked against the wall. I lift a light scarf and then let it go. The scarf falls turn after turn, and it does not hit anything.

Then comes our turn. From sitting we lean back slowly, the way the scarf came down: hands first, then elbows, then your back, one vertebra after another, until you touch the cushions. You bounce back a little and then settle, and the cushions sink under your back as much as your weight. We lie down, with your eyes closed or open as you like.

Your chest rises and falls, and the cushions under you are still. Near us on the floor the scarf lies flat.

16. A Breath Finds Its Order

نَفْخَةٌ تَنْتَظِمُ

On the table is a thin sheet of paper the size of your palm. You blow a small puff on it, and its edge dances but it does not fly.

On the first try you blow a strong puff, and the paper flies off the table and lands on the floor. You laugh and pick it up. After an ordinary breath, on the second try, you put your hand on your belly and blow a longer, gentler breath, and the edge of the paper rises and trembles. On the third your belly draws in a little and the air leaves steadily, and the paper stays hanging at the edge of flight. You leave the paper and sit, with your palm on your chest.

The paper is still on the table, and its edge is still tilted a little, as if remembering.

17. The Lean of the Trunk

مَيْلُ الْجِذْعِ

You stand sideways to the wall, an arm’s length from it. A yellow strip of cloth has its top end taped to the wall at the level of your shoulder, and its bottom end hangs free. The game is for your trunk to lean toward it while your pelvis stays in place, and for you to ask your trunk, “Where is your end?”

You lean toward the strip and stretch your fingers. Before you reach it your body begins to wobble, and your pelvis slides to the other side. You plant your feet and lean again slowly, and your ribs open on the far side like a fan, and you touch the strip with the tip of a finger. You return to the middle and relax your arms.

The free end of the yellow strip sways from your touch and then settles on the wall.

18. Near the Source

قُرْبُ الْمَصْدَرِ

In a dim room there is a small lamp on the table, giving off a gentle warmth. The narrator sees the whole room: the lamp, the table, and your steps. You take a step toward the lamp, then another, and you come closer.

You step back and laugh. Then you move forward half a step. There, about an arm and a half from the light, where does the warmth live? You stay where you are and close your eyes if you wish.

You open your eyes. On the wall behind you is a big shadow of you, and around its head a thin ring of light.

19. Tracking the Speed

تَتَبُّعُ السُّرْعَةِ

A red dot on the tip of a pen that an adult moves in front of you, from right to left. Your head is still, and your eyes chase it. It is faster than you, so you miss it.

You laugh, “Again!” This time the dot runs slower. And suddenly your eyes jump to the end of the path a moment before the dot, and wait for it there. Does anything change between your eyebrows?

The dot comes back, faster than before. And your eyes have beaten it to the corner, and are still waiting.

20. The Sound of the Drop

صَوْتُ الْقَطْرَةِ

I sit on a stable chair near the sink. Water comes down from the tap, drop after drop, into a metal bowl. I close my eyes, and nothing is left but the sound.

I count: one, two, three, and a drop gets lost from me. I stumble and laugh, and I stop counting. The next drop falls without my catching it. I exhale with the landing of each drop, and my chest goes down with it, and the counting happens by itself.

I open my eyes. In the bowl a small ring widens on the surface of the water, and a new drop falls into it.

21. The Pebble Slips

زَلَقُ الْحَصَاةِ

A smooth pebble from a beach, which you washed with water and soap. It is still wet in your palm, and the water shines on it like a layer of glass.

You squeeze it hard, and it slips from between your fingers and leaps like a small fish onto the spread towel. You pick it up, open your palm like a bowl, and relax your fingers. The pebble settles in the middle of the palm and does not move.

The water runs from the pebble into the lines of your palm, then stops. After a minute one side of the pebble dries and becomes lighter in color, and the other side stays dark.

22. The Taste Dissolves

ذَوَبَانُ الطَّعْمِ

A small mint tablet on the tip of your tongue. No chewing and no biting, and you sit upright.

A sharp tingle, like a cold spark! You smile from the surprise, and you let the tablet dissolve and do not move it. The tingle softens, and something cool and sweet spreads over your tongue and the roof of your mouth.

You swallow your saliva gently and draw a calm breath through your nose. The air turns cool like a breeze at dawn, and the trace of the mint stays on your tongue until it disappears.

23. The Way the Gust Leans

اتِّجَاهُ الْهَبَّةِ

The end of a light scarf in your hand, and the rest of it left to the air. An open window behind your back, which you cannot see.

You hold the end of the scarf in a tight fist. A gust passes, and the scarf swells, then falls back and taps your hand with its tip, as if it were joking with you.

You loosen your fingers a little, and the end stays in your palm. A light pull comes, then eases, then arrives from another side. Each gust draws your wrist toward one direction, and your wrist knows the direction before your eyes do.

You let the end go where the gust pushes it. The scarf leans forward, away from your back. You turn around, and you see the window curtain swelling like a sail.

24. The Flash of Light

وَمِيضُ الضَّوْءِ

A patch of dim light from a flashlight on a dark wall. A friend holds the flashlight and moves the patch slowly from one corner to another, and never points the light at the eyes.

The first time, you stare at the flash as if you were catching it with your eyes. It slips into the corner while your eyelids stay tight.

The second time, you chase it with your whole head, so your neck twists and the light gets ahead of you.

The third time, you let your eyes move without strain. The flash passes along the wall, and your eyes receive it from the edge of your sight. Does something behind your eyebrows ease?

You close your eyes for one second, then open them. The light stands in the far corner, and on the wall beside you is the shadow of a picture frame you had not noticed before.

25. Beats After Running

دَقَّاتُ مَا بَعْدَ الْجَرْيِ

Running in place for fifteen seconds on a dry floor. Then standing, a palm on the chest, and a clock on the wall ticking.

I run in place, then stop suddenly. I put my palm on my chest, and something fast knocks beneath it: دَقَّ دَقَّ دَقَّ, knock, knock, knock!

I let out a long breath. A beat, then a small gap, then a beat. The gaps between the beats grow wider under my palm, and on the wall the clock goes on ticking without hurry.

I lower my hand and listen to the clock. Then I place two fingers on my wrist and find the beat there too, a little faster than the ticking of the clock, and growing closer to it.

26. The Edge Between Two Surfaces

حَافَّةُ السَّطْحَيْنِ

An edge between a flat rug and a tile floor. Bare feet, and a nearby wall that your palm can reach.

You step onto the edge quickly, and your foot shakes and your leg wobbles, and your palm touches the wall.

You go back to the edge. You stand. You wait a little. You shift your weight slowly from the rug to the tile. Under one foot is the softness of the rug, under the other is the coolness of the tile, and something in the sole of your foot finds the ground.

You stay standing with one foot on each surface. Your fingers move away from the wall, centimeter after centimeter.

27. The Boat’s Wave

مَوْجَةُ الْقَارِبِ

A wide basin with a little water, on a table, with a towel under it. A paper boat floats in its middle.

You touch the water’s surface with a fingertip, and a small ring spreads out and passes under the boat, so it hardly moves.

You move your palm back and forth, a push and then a wait, a push and then a wait. If the push matches the return of the wave, the wave grows and carries the boat to the other side, then brings it back. And if the push comes before it, the waves collide, and spray flies onto your sleeve.

You hold the boat still with a fingertip. The last wave passes under it, strikes the edge of the basin, and comes back to your finger as a small ring.

28. The Shadow Stops

وَقْفَةُ الظِّلِّ

A wide flat ground under the afternoon sun, and a long shadow that runs ahead of you, because the sun is behind you.

You walk fast to step on your shadow’s head, and the shadow is always one step ahead. You speed up a little, and it speeds up too.

Then you stop. Your shadow stops at the same moment, still in front of you as if it were waiting. With every inhale your ribs widen and the shadow of your chest rises, and with every exhale it sinks a little.

A thin cloud passes, and the shadow fades, then returns to its sharpness, while you stand where you are.

29. An Echo and the Shake in the Throat

صَدًى وَرَجَّةُ الْحَلْقِ

A light clap. Fingertips on the throat. A small hum with a closed mouth.

I am an echo. I was born from a light clap between your palms.

I went out into the air, and no one could see me anymore. Did I go away?

You place your fingers on your throat and hum with a closed mouth. Under your fingers is a small shake, and the bone of your jaw carries it to your ears from the inside. I am not that shake, but I know it: we are both vibration.

I did not vanish all at once. A part of me came back from the wall after a moment, weaker than I was and farther away, and then I dissolved into the air of the room.

Your shake, though, lasts as long as you hum, and settles when you stop. And your fingers remember their place on your throat.

The wall goes quiet, and your fingers stay on your throat.

30. The Seed That Refused to Hurry

الْبَذْرَةُ الَّتِي رَفَضَتِ الْعَجَلَةَ

Sitting in a squat. Palms open on clean, damp soil. Pressing gently, as if asking permission.

In the garden of the house, in the morning. Kalila squats beside a small hole where she buried a radish seed yesterday. She digs with a wooden spoon until the seed shows, looks at it, then buries it and digs again.

Kalila says: “A whole day has passed over you, little seed, and you are still stalling.”

Sindbad stands behind her with his chin raised: “I am Sindbad, and I have seen seven seas waiting for the wind. The root goes down first in the dark, and then the stem comes up. Every time you dig it up, you cut off its road. Put your palms on the soil gently.”

Kalila puts her palms on the soil and presses gently. The soil is damp. It sinks a little under her palms, then holds firm.

Kalila says: “It seems the seed has a schedule, and it has not sent me a copy.”

Kalila smooths the soil with her palms and gives it a little water from a small cup. Nothing comes up at that moment.

After three days a small green stem comes up with two leaves. Sindbad says without turning around: “I told you.” And Kalila says: “And you said it three times.” Then she stares at the stem with her hands behind her back.

31. A Talk Between a Palm and a Weight

حِوَارٌ بَيْنَ كَفٍّ وَثِقَلٍ

A soft rope tied to a small bag of pebbles on flat ground. A slow pull. A palm that observes the weight.

Sindbad sat on the ground and held the end of the rope in his palm. At the other end lay the bag of pebbles, two meters away.

He pulled hard. The bag jumped to one side and spun to the other, and the rope pulled his palm sideways.

Sindbad said, with his chest puffed out: “I am Sindbad, who dragged a ship to the shore. So why does a bag of pebbles disobey me?”

Kalila said from a distance: “The sea was helping you, Sindbad, but the bag flatters no one.”

Only the weight answered, by staying where it was.

Sindbad pulled with the exhale, paused with the inhale, and loosened his palm a little. The bag slid toward him in a straight line, and the rope no longer twisted his palm.

The bag tapped the tip of his shoe once, and went still.

32. Hands That Make Room for Emptiness

يَدَانِ تَتَّسِعَانِ لِلْفَرَاغِ

Two palms cupped together like a vessel, with a small space between them. A deep breath.

You look at your hands. There is nothing in them, and yet they look as if they are waiting.

You blow a warm breath into your palms, which have gaps between their fingers, and the warmth passes through the gaps and is lost.

You bring the fingers together without pressure, and blow once more. Where does the warmth sit?

Your hands look after this small warmth for as long as the fingers are gently closed.

You open your palms upward, and the air of the room passes over them, a little cool.

33. The First Dot

النُّقْطَةُ الْأُولَى

A raised pen over a white sheet. A deep breath. A slow descent with the exhale.

A white sheet, and a pen in my palm that grows heavy before it touches it.

It rises, then lowers a centimeter, then rises. My fingers ask: where do I begin? Every place will do, and no place seems to me like a first.

I exhale, and the pen comes down.

A dot. Small as a raindrop, black, wet for a moment.

My hand begins to move, and the line comes out of the dot as if it had been waiting for it.

And the sheet is white all over, except here.

34. At the Edge

عِنْدَ الْحَدِّ

Two index fingers face each other on the table, a hand’s width apart.

The boy: “I’ll bring my finger closer until it reaches your finger.”

The girl: “Bring it slowly, and leave a hair of air between us.”

The finger moves forward from a hand’s width to two fingers’ width, then to the length of one finger joint. The boy lets out a short laugh, then stops.

The boy: “Something is getting warm at the tip of my finger, and we haven’t touched yet.”

The girl: “The warmth arrived before your hand did.”

They breathe together, in and then out, and the two fingers stay where they are. In the small gap between them a gentle warmth stands, like a thread no one can see.

35. What I Cannot See

مَا لَا أَرَاهُ

A thin ribbon of cloth between two fingers, by an open window.

The cloth is in my hand. Still.

The air cannot be seen. There is no sign on the road.

Then the end of the ribbon trembles. It bends. It returns. It bends.

Now I know that something passed, and my eyes saw nothing of it.

The ribbon goes still. I wait.

I blow a gentle puff from my lips. The ribbon trembles again.

The wind that moved it came out of my chest.

36. The Sound of “Here”

صَوْتُ «هُنَا»

Large smooth stones on a board, and one stone between the fingers.

Sindbad hurries, and the stone rolls far away.

Sindbad: “Oh, why won’t it stay in the row?”

Kalila: “Because your hand runs ahead of your ear, Sindbad. Put it down, then listen.”

Sindbad slows down. The stone touches the board and gives a small tap.

Sindbad: “A tap. As if it says: here.”

Kalila: “Bring the second one after it.”

The stones line up one after another, and a steady sound rises in the room: نَقْ, نَقْ, نَقْ, which sounds like naq, naq, naq.

Sindbad counts on his finger to the rhythm. Kalila puts the last stone at the end of the row, and then a stillness settles that feels like a tap that has not happened yet.

37. A Mark in the Air

أَثَرٌ فِي الْهَوَاءِ

A spray of clean water on the back of the hand, and an index finger drawing in the air above it.

A spritz on the back of my hand, cold and small and shiny.

I lift my finger and draw: a loop, then a wave, then a circle.

The droplets on my skin say رِشْ, رِشْ, like rish, rish.

I did not draw on paper. I drew in the air, and where my finger passed, the mist cooled a little more.

The water dries drop after drop, and the cold disappears from the back of my hand spot after spot.

38. Listen Downward

أَنْصِتِي إِلَى أَسْفَلَ

Standing on ground that is level and not slippery, with a palm resting on the knee.

I used to think climbing was a ladder I folded upward in a single step.

I raised my hand proudly: “I made it!” and I swayed.

Sindbad asked me: “Where is your weight now, Kalila?”

I said: “In my head.”

Sindbad said: “Listen downward first.”

I went down to my feet and found my toes spreading on the floor, my two heels heavy, and the sole of the foot tightening, then letting go.

I handed my weight to the floor, then climbed rung by rung: the legs, then the pelvis, then the back, then the shoulders.

At the last rung my head was light, and my heels were still carrying their weight on the ground.

39. The Smell That Surprises

الرَّائِحَةُ تُفَاجِئُ

Three small samples on a tray: an orange peel, a mint leaf, and a piece of wet clay pottery. A calm sniff from a hand’s distance.

Entry One. I smell the peel. Sweet and sharp, it rises to my nose quickly.

Entry Two. I smell the mint leaf. Cool, as if the air in my nose has grown wider.

Entry Three. I wait for another perfume, but the piece of pottery gives off the smell of earth after rain! I laugh, and I smell again.

The scent rises with my breath to the top of my nose, stays there for a moment, then pulls back with the exhale.

At the end of the log I write one line: The rain lives in the clay.

40. Layer After Layer

طَبَقَةٌ بَعْدَ طَبَقَةٍ

A small, soft piece of washed fruit on the tip of the tongue, with the person seated and settled in their chair.

I place the piece on my tongue, and I do not chew.

First a sourness pinches my tongue. My lips tighten, and saliva flows in my mouth.

Then the saliva mixes with the piece and it softens. The sourness withdraws to the side, and a quiet sweetness wells up.

I swallow slowly. Nothing is left in my mouth, and still a delicate taste lingers at the back of my mouth.

41. A Sound in the Chest

صَوْتٌ فِي الصَّدْرِ

An empty wooden box on the table, and a palm waiting on the chest.

Sindbad hits the box with his fist: طُمْ, tum!

Sindbad: “Ouch! What an awful sound!”

Kalila: “Because you’re fighting it, Sindbad. Tap it with your fingertips.”

Sindbad taps gently. A round whisper runs through the wood.

Sindbad: “The shaking climbs up my arm! And it reaches my shoulder!”

Kalila: “Now put your palm on your chest and hum: هُمْ, hum.”

Sindbad hums: هُمْ. His chest shakes under his hand like another box.

Kalila laughs, and the wooden box laughs with her, and so does the bigger box under Sindbad’s palm.

42. The Widening Eye

الْعَيْنُ الَّتِي تَتَّسِعُ

A steady gaze at the tip of a pen an arm’s length away for five seconds, then a look at the farthest point in the room or out the window.

Your eye is on the pen tip, and the world narrows until it becomes a dot. Your forehead tightens, and your eyebrows draw close.

Then you move your gaze to the far wall, and the view opens like a door, and a breath goes out of you: هَاهْ, haah.

Your eye does not chase details, and lets the view come.

The far edge of the window, the roof of the house opposite, a single color in the sky, and between your eyebrows, does a plain open out?

43. A Palm That Holds Both Opposites

رَاحَةٌ تَسَعُ الضِّدَّيْنِ

The right hand on a lukewarm, smooth cup, and the left hand on a cold, rough stone.

The First Hand. It touches the lukewarm cup. Smooth, and warm like the water of wudu, and the warmth runs into the fingers.

The Second Hand. It touches the stone and recoils from its cold, then returns to it little by little.

The two hands side by side, and the skin carries two reports at one moment.

The hands trade places. The warm right hand goes to the stone, and it seems colder than it seemed to the left. And the left hand enters the cup’s warmth, and it seems warmer than it seemed to the right.

The trace of the cold stays in the left palm for a few breaths, and the trace of the warmth in the right.

44. The Ankle’s Dance

رَقْصَةُ الْكَاحِلِ

Standing on one foot beside a wall, with the eyes open on a fixed point.

Your eyes are open on a fixed point, and the whole world sways a little.

You lean to one side and almost set your foot down, then you come back. Your body searches for equilibrium.

Your leg does not stiffen, and your ankle moves right and left in very small movements, and the weight shifts with it.

Then the foot comes down to the floor, and touches the firm surface beneath it.

45. The Waiting Air

الْهَوَاءُ الَّذِي يَنْتَظِرُ

You sit upright with your feet on the floor. You take a calm breath, no holding, and count three counts on your fingers with it.

Your chest fills like a small balloon. You count: one, two.

At three, the air waits a small moment at the door of your chest, and you do not hold it. You open the door for it, and the air leaves gently, and your shoulders come down.

Once more: a calm breath, no holding, then two counts, then you blow gently on a thin paper ribbon that you hold a hand-span from your mouth. The ribbon trembles and bends, then goes still again.

46. A Sting, Then Warmth

لَسْعَةٌ ثُمَّ دِفْءٌ

Two open hands in front of the chest. One strong clap, then one light clap.

Sindbad claps with all his strength, and the place rings and his palms turn red.

Sindbad: “Ouch! It feels as if my hands were stung!”

Kalila: “And your sound, Sindbad, is like a duck beating the water with its wings. What if you brought your hands close to your cheek first?”

Sindbad claps lightly, and a small breeze blows on his face.

Sindbad: “This is a light sting, and after it comes warmth!”

Sindbad puts his hands on his cheeks. The sting eases little by little, and a warmth stays, like the warmth of a cup between two palms.

Kalila claps one last time, lightly, and they hear a round sound like a raindrop.

47. The Bow That Does Not Break

الِانْحِنَاءُ الَّذِي لَا يَكْسِرُ

Feet apart at the width of the shoulders, and knees soft. The chin lowers slowly toward the chest.

You bend like a palm tree heavy with ripe dates.

The head goes down first, and the shoulders follow.

One vertebra, then another vertebra, then another,

and the arms hang down with nothing to do.

Behind your knees a gentle pull begins, like a thread being drawn tight. Here you stop.

You rise vertebra by vertebra. The back first, then the shoulders, and the head last of all.

You stand, with your head above your shoulders and steady ground beneath your feet.

48. The Throat’s Secret

سِرُّ الْحَلْقِ

One hand on the throat, with light fingers that do not press. One word is said aloud, then whispered.

I put my fingers on my throat and say aloud: “Water.” My fingers buzz as if a bee had landed on them.

My little brother says: “Now with the secret!”

I whisper: “Water.” The buzzing goes away. Nothing is left under my fingers but stillness, and a small warm breeze comes out of my mouth.

My brother puts his fingers on his throat and tries, then laughs: “It vanished! This is a secret only someone who has put a hand there can know.”

We switch between the loud voice and the whisper three times with a new word, and our fingers know the difference before our ears do.

The last word we whisper, then we lower our hands to our knees and fall silent. Our throats stay still, and a warm, calm breath comes out of us.

49. Holding the Stone

مَسْكَةُ الْحَجَرِ

A smooth stone the size of an egg in the palm of the hand. The fingers close around it over a spread towel.

The Hand: “I will hold you with all my strength!”

The fingers tighten until the knuckles turn white.

The Stone: “I slip when I am squeezed, like a wet bar of soap.”

The stone slips and falls onto the towel with a muffled sound. The owner of the hand looks at their empty palm.

The hand picks it up again and loosens its fingers until they barely touch it.

The Stone: “This is how I stay. There is a place for me in the palm.”

The whole hand opens. The stone sits in the middle of the palm, with a trace of warmth from the fingers still in it.

50. The Push of Steadiness

دَفْعَةُ الثَّبَاتِ

Standing on a non-slip floor facing an empty wall, with one step separating the feet from it, and two palms on it.

You pushed the wall with your arms alone. Your shoulders rose toward your ears, your neck grew tired, and the wall did not move.

Then you pressed your heels into the floor. The push rose from the heels to the knees, then to the back, then to your palms. Your shoulders came down, and your body became a bridge between the floor and the wall.

You pushed the wall gently until your trunk was upright, then lifted your palms off it. Your body stayed standing for a moment as if the push had not ended, then your weight returned to your feet.

51. Turning the Torso

لَيُّ الْجِذْعِ

Standing, hands on the waist, and feet planted and not turning. The trunk twists slowly to the right, then the left.

I look at a door in front of me and then twist my trunk slowly to the right. My eyes go ahead of my head, my head goes ahead of my chest, and my waist follows last of all. I see a side of the room I was not seeing, and my trunk stops at the first pull in my waist.

Then I twist to the left at the same slow pace and compare the two sides.

I come back to the front, and the door I looked at is still in its place.

52. A Jump That Absorbs

وَثْبَةٌ تَمْتَصُّ

Feet on a carpet or a mat. Knees bending slightly. A small jump that lifts the feet only the width of two fingers.

You jump. You land with your knees stiff. Clack! The impact climbs from your heels to your teeth, and your body shakes like a taut pole.

You jump again. You bend your knees before you touch the ground and after. Hss. The sound turns muffled, like a foot on sand, and the impact is lost in your knees.

Five small jumps with soft knees, each one quieter than the one before.

You stand. You hear your breath calm down, and your feet stay on the carpet.

53. Swimming Without Water

سِبَاحَةٌ بِلَا مَاءٍ

Sitting on a chair or standing on firm ground, with eyes open. Two arms drawing slow circles like the arms of a swimmer.

The girl moves her arms as if she were swimming in thick water. A soft sound comes from her sleeves: wush, wush.

After two turns her breath joins her arms without her asking it to. Her arms widen with the in-breath like two wings and return with the out-breath, until her chest becomes a single wave passing from her belly to her shoulders.
