Section 5 · Beneath the Surface

Water runs above the depths, and the depths are in no hurry.

21. The Dog at the Mouth of the Cave

الْكَلْبُ عِنْدَ فَمِ الْكَهْفِ

The rain pecked at the rock of the cave as if many fingers were tapping it. Sindbad and Kalila came in, shaking the water from their clothes, and the smell of wet earth filled the place.

At the mouth of the cave a big dog lay on an old mat with his chin on his paws. He did not bark and he did not sleep. Beside him stood a clay bowl of water, and a piece of dry bread he had not touched.

Sindbad said, “I can’t bear waiting. I’ll sleep until this rain stops.”

Kalila said, “And he? Isn’t he waiting?”

“He’s a dog. He doesn’t know how to get bored.” And Sindbad held out a crumb from his pocket. The dog sniffed it politely, then put his chin back on his paws.

Kalila said, “He didn’t refuse your crumb. He’s simply busy.”

“Look at his ears.”

Sindbad looked. Whenever the roar of the wind rose or a stone rolled, the dog’s ears stayed still. It was as if he waited for one sound in particular and let all the others go by.

Sindbad wanted to do the same, and could not. He counted the drops, scratched his nose, sighed three times, and twisted his turban around a finger until it went red. Then he tired of moving and fell quiet, and leaned against the cold wall.

At that moment he heard, beneath the rain, a small broken ringing: a bell, then a bell, then heavy steps in the mud.

The dog leapt up and dashed outside, his tail beating against the rock. After a little he came back, followed by an old man wrapped in a wet cloak, driving two small goats before him.

The old man said, “Peace be upon you both. My dog never forgets me, not even in the longest rains.”

Then he opened his satchel and broke a warm loaf into three pieces. Sindbad took his piece and sat near the dog, who laid his head on his master’s knee.

When everyone had eaten, the old man sat shaking the rain from his beard, and said to Sindbad, “I heard my bell ringing in the mud, and nothing else.” Sindbad laughed, for he had heard the bell only after he fell silent.

And Sindbad saw the dog’s ear turn toward him with every mouthful he chewed.

Activity

Sit with someone in your family, or with your teacher, for two minutes without talking. Choose a single sound from the sounds around you and follow it with your ears. Afterwards tell the person beside you which sound you chose, and whether it stayed with you or got lost.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad notice about the dog’s ears?
  2. What do you do when you are waiting for something that is late?

22. The Knot on the Well Rope

الْعُقْدَةُ عَلَى حَبْلِ الْبِئْرِ

Sindbad returned to the village where he had grown up, and found the well in its place. Its stones were smooth and cool under his hand, the pulley creaked as it always had, and from its depths rose the smell of deep water and damp clay.

He had expected his heart to tighten. But something else took his attention: a little girl lowering the bucket, which flipped over in the air and came back empty. She did it once, and twice, and three times.

She said, blowing a lock of hair off her forehead, “It won’t fill. I don’t know why.”

Sindbad said confidently, “I tighten sails in storms. Let me.” He took the rope and lowered the bucket, and it flipped in the air and came back empty, just as it had for her.

He was quiet for a moment and looked at the rope, and remembered his grandfather’s rough hands. His grandfather used to tie a knot at the end of the rope, and the bucket would tilt and dip and fill.

He said, “Wait. I think my hands know something that I don’t.”

His fingers wound the rope twice, passed the end under the loop, and pulled tight. His head did not remember the steps, but his fingers knew them.

The bucket went down on its side, and they heard the gurgle of water entering it, and the rope shivered in Sindbad’s palm like a live fish. When it rose, heavy, the children clapped.

The girl said, “Who taught you?”

“My grandfather.”

“And who will teach me?”

Sindbad laughed, and took her small hand in his, and tied the knot again, slowly. From the shade of the wall Kalila muttered, licking her paw, “At last your hands have found work worth doing.”

And Kalila said to the girl, “Don’t watch his hand. Feel the rope.” The girl tried on her own. The first time the knot came out crooked. By the fourth it held, and she drew up the bucket with both hands, scooped water into her palm and drank, the water running down her wrist.

Sindbad stood watching the wet rope in her grip, its drops falling on his sandals one after another.

Activity

Take a thick string and a spoon, and tie the string around the end of the spoon in any way you like. Then teach someone in your family, or a classmate, a small job you do well with your hands, and see how their hands learn.

Questions for the guide

  1. What kept happening to the bucket before Sindbad tied the knot, and what changed afterwards?
  2. What is a small job your hands know that you learned from someone you love?

23. The River That Changed Its Course

النَّهْرُ الَّذِي غَيَّرَ مَجْرَاهُ

Sindbad woke to something missing. He did not know what it was, until he understood: the river had gone silent.

The caravan had slept on its bank, and all night the river’s rumble had been like a grandfather whispering a story. Now there was no water, and the camels sniffed the air and groaned. A wide bed of shining pebbles and cracked mud remained, and one lone shell glinted in the sun.

Sindbad shouted, “The river is dead! I will find whoever killed it.”

Kalila said, stretching, “And who do you think did it? The sun? The pebbles?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then begin at the top. Water doesn’t leave its road unless something cuts across it.”

They walked along the dry riverbed, Sindbad, Kalila, and Ali, who carried two empty waterskins on his shoulders. The pebbles clattered under their feet, and in the mud were the prints of a long-legged bird. After an hour they saw what had not been there yesterday: a huge heap of earth and rock blocking the gap between two hills. In the night the rain had brought down the side of a hill.

Ali pushed the heap with his shoulder until his face went red, and not one pebble moved. Sindbad came to help, and they pushed together until the sweat ran off them, and a handful of gravel fell on their feet.

Ali said, panting, “I am stronger than this heap. It has to move.”

Kalila said, “And water is weaker than you, and still it did not stay here. The answer isn’t over there, Ali. Look to the left.”

On the left the water had found a lower edge, and slipped among the tamarisk trees, shining and winding, carrying a yellow leaf like a boat.

Ali set down the waterskins and smiled, wiping his forehead. Sindbad pulled off his sandals and dipped his feet in the cold water, and cried out as if something had stung him.

When he calmed, a blue dragonfly landed on his wet toe, and stayed there.

Activity

Walk with a grown-up from your front door to the farthest safe place you both know, and come back by a different way. Draw the two routes on a sheet of paper, and compare what you saw on each.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did they find at the top of the river, and what did the water do when its way was blocked?
  2. What did you do when you found a closed door and there was another door beside it?

24. The Palm That Could Not See Her Feet

النَّخْلَةُ الَّتِي لَمْ تَرَ قَدَمَيْهَا

At the head of the valley stood a palm tree that could see the sea, and saw caravans before they arrived, and saw the sun as it climbed. Every morning she told the wind what she had seen, and the wind passed by without answering.

Below her, among the roots, an ant was dragging a piece of dry bread ten times bigger than herself, stumbling over every pebble.

The palm said, “You there, little one, what can you see from down there? Dirt? I see the whole world.”

The ant put down the bread and wiped her brow with her foreleg. She knew how to answer the grown-ups without anger.

The ant lifted her head a little and said, “Very well. And you? Can you see your feet?”

The palm fell silent. She tried to look down at herself and could not bend her neck. She shook her fronds in a temper, and dozens of ripe dates dropped to the ground and rolled among the cracks and holes.

Kalila, passing by, said, “What a waste! The crows will eat them.”

But the ant had already called her sisters with a touch of her antennae. A long line came crawling like a black thread. Each one pushed or dragged a date and disappeared with it into a crack that no eye could see from above. The sweet smell of dates filled the place, and the palm heard many small rustlings at her trunk.

From the village came children, running, gathering what was left of the dates, laughing and flicking the stones across the sand. Kalila said to them, “Leave the ants their share, for they have worked harder than you,” and each child set a date by the crack.

The palm said in a small voice, “I never knew there were all these beneath me.”

Kalila said, “And they never knew there was someone above them who could see the sea.”

The palm leaned a little in the gentle, warm wind of the afternoon, and let one frond hang down to touch the back of the last child who passed beneath her, and he turned to look up.

Activity

Sit on the ground in a garden or a yard for three minutes, and look only at what is near your feet. Draw with a pencil one thing you could not see while you were standing.

Questions for the guide

  1. What happened to the dates when they fell, and who came to gather them?
  2. What can you see from a high place, and what from a low place?

25. Juha and His Serious Donkey

جُحَا وَحِمَارُهُ الْجَادُّ

Juha sat on his donkey facing the tail and rode into the market.

Sindbad, who had caught up with him at the market gate, said, “You’re the wrong way round, Juha.”

“Me? It is the world that’s the wrong way round. I look at what I have passed, and he looks at what is to come. That way nothing is lost to us.”

The donkey walked with slow dignity, never turning, not a twitch in his ears. Everyone who passed laughed, except him. The market was crowded with the smell of bread and cinnamon, and the watermelon seller called out in a voice like a trumpet.

Juha said, patting the donkey’s neck, “This is a serious donkey. He doesn’t laugh. I’ve told him thirty jokes and he hasn’t smiled.”

Sindbad said proudly, “I can make him laugh. Listen.” He stood before the donkey and began to tell of his voyages, and of the whale he had wrestled with his two bare hands. The bigger the story grew, the more Sindbad stepped back to show them the size of the whale.

Juha whispered to the people, “Hush. The artist is at work.” As for the donkey, he looked at the imaginary whale with heavy eyelids, chewed something unseen, and flicked his tail at a fly.

Behind Sindbad was a heap of straw.

Sindbad fell on his back, legs in the air, turban over his nose, straw flying all around him. The watermelon seller laughed, and a boy carrying a basket of eggs laughed, and a hen landed on his belly in astonishment.

Then they heard a long, broken bray, like an old door opening and shutting a hundred times. The donkey was shaking from his head to his hooves.

Sindbad lay there a moment, furious, and then found that he was laughing too. He laughed until his eyes filled with tears.

Juha said, wiping his eyes, “Thirty jokes didn’t work, and one tumble did.”

Sindbad got up, dusting off his clothes. The donkey stretched out his neck, pulled a stalk of straw from the turban with his teeth, and chewed it calmly.

Activity

Tell your brother or a friend a short funny story about yourself, about a day when you found yourself in an amusing spot. Then listen to a story from them, and notice when you laugh together.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Sindbad try in order to make the donkey laugh, and what made it laugh in the end?
  2. Have you ever laughed at yourself in front of your friends?

Section 6 · What the Wind and the Stars Say

An open sky, and an ear that listens.

26. The Wind That Does Not Speak

الرِّيحُ الَّتِي لَا تَتَكَلَّمُ

The people of the Clay Village cannot hear the wind when it comes. Their houses are smooth walls without trees or flags or shutters that bang, so the wind passes without a sound, and they never know when it starts or when it grows strong.

Sindbad, Talia and Kalila reached the village at noon, and found the doors shut and the jars covered. An old woman said from behind a crack, “Whenever dust shows on the horizon, we hide. The wind is silent, and we don’t know what it wants.”

Sindbad said, “It doesn’t want anything. I’m standing in it right now and it hasn’t done a thing to me.” But at that moment his red kerchief flew off, and he ran thirty paces after it.

Talia stood still, listening. She wet a finger and raised it, and felt cold on one side of it. She saw the dust slide along the ground without a whisper. She said, “The wind has no sound of its own. Its sound is the sound of everything it passes through.”

Talia smiled like someone who has understood a riddle, and walked to the canal. She gathered dry reeds from its bank, cut three pipes from them, and hung them on a string with the small shells she had in her pocket. She tied it all to a long pole on the roof of the nearest house.

At first they heard a faint clicking, shell touching shell. Then the wind rose, and a thin whistle came from the reed pipes. The old woman heard it and put her head out of the door with a ladle in her hand.

Talia said to the people, who had opened their doors a little, “If it clicks, it’s a breeze. If it whistles, cover the jars.”

And Kalila said to Sindbad drily, “At last, a wind that talks less than you.”

For days the village lived listening to the pole. Every morning the children gathered beneath it to ask, “Clicking or whistling?” And in the evening Sindbad sat under it with his dusty kerchief on his knees, counting the clicks of the shells.

Activity

Near an open window, hang a ribbon, a small bell, or a few spoons tied to a string. Sit beside it and listen to the sounds the wind makes, and compare light air with strong air.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did the people of the village not know when the wind came, and what did Talia do?
  2. Which sounds in your home tell you something?

27. The Whale Against the Waves

الْحُوتُ فِي وَجْهِ الْمَوْجِ

The boat rose and fell, the salt stung Sindbad’s lips, and the wind was driving them toward black rocks. On the boat an old fisherman held the oar, and Ali helped him, his hands red from gripping.

Suddenly a huge grey back appeared, and blew a spout of mist with a sound like a great bellows. A whale was swimming against all the waves, toward the rocks.

Sindbad said, “Mad! I know the sea. No one swims like that.”

Kalila said, clinging to the sail rope with her eyes half shut, “Perhaps you know the sea only above the surface. Follow him, and don’t judge.”

Sindbad muttered, “I’ll follow him, but if we drown it’s his fault.”

The fisherman turned the oar and said to Ali, “Don’t row until I tell you.” Ali longed to push with all his strength, but he waited. They followed the whale at a distance. The waves struck them, and Sindbad clenched his teeth and grumbled.

Then, at the head of the black rock, the sea suddenly softened. The whale entered a small sheltered bay whose water was still as glass, with the rocks standing as a wall between it and the wind. A gull cried above the rocks, and they heard the sound of the waves receding behind them as though a door had been closed.

Ali breathed deeply. The fisherman said quietly, “This is the harbour of the north wind. My grandfather used to say the whales know it.”

The water among the rocks at the edge of the bay was draining away with the ebb. There, in a shallow pool, Sindbad saw a small silver fish beating its tail against the stone and finding no way out.

He stepped into the shallow water, the fisherman holding his robe from behind, and scooped her up in his hands and returned her to the deep water, and she slipped between his fingers like a cold ribbon. The fisherman said, “None of us saw her but you.”

Far off, outside the bay, a white column rose from the whale’s breath, and Sindbad’s palm stayed wet and dripping.

Activity

Draw on a sheet of paper a map of a sea with wind and rocks and a sheltered bay, then show it to someone in your family and ask where they would put their boat if a storm came.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did the whale do when it swam against the waves, and what did Sindbad find when he followed it?
  2. Have you ever seen someone do something strange and then understood the reason?

28. Scheherazade and the Blank Page

شَهْرَزَادُ وَالصَّفْحَةُ الْبَيْضَاءُ

Under a fig tree sat Scheherazade, with a huge book on her knees and a pen behind her ear. Its last page was blank. The air under the tree was warm with the smell of leaves, and a bee circled a split fig.

Sindbad, his bag on his shoulder, said, “I have passed you three times, and the page is the same. When will you finish the book?”

“When the story ends.”

“And when does it end?”

“I don’t know. It is still happening.”

Sindbad set down his bag. “I have bigger stories than this one: a whale, a storm, an island that walks.” Scheherazade said, “Keep the island for another time. Today I want your morning.”

Sindbad grew annoyed, and sat beside her, puffing. From among the fig leaves a ripe fig dropped into his lap, and he ate it, and found nothing to say.

Scheherazade said, “Tell me what you did this morning.”

“Nothing important.”

“Try.”

He said reluctantly, “I woke. I broke a loaf. I saw a dog sleeping in the street and stepped over his tail so as not to wake him... Then I saw a woman selling figs, and she said good morning to me, and I did not answer her.”

Scheherazade asked him, “And the dog? What colour was he?” He said, “Grey, with a scar on his ear.” And he was astonished that he remembered.

Scheherazade wrote with every sentence. When he finished, she turned the book toward him and said, “Read.”

Sindbad looked, expecting two cold lines, and found his day written there in her fine hand, down to the dog’s tail and the morning loaf. And he noticed that the page said the woman had been smiling, though he had never told her so.

He took the pen from behind her ear carefully, like someone picking up a butterfly. It was lighter than he had thought. He stood a moment looking at the last sentence, then added something in a wobbly hand. Scheherazade did not ask him what he had written.

Scheherazade closed the book with a single fig leaf between the pages, and put the pen back behind her ear.

Activity

Take a notebook and write on one page what happened to you today, with its small details: what you saw, what you heard, what you ate. Begin with a sentence like “In the morning...” and go on in your own way. Then leave a blank page for tomorrow.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Scheherazade ask Sindbad, and what did he find in the book after he answered?
  2. What small thing happened to you today that you would like to see written down?

29. The Rain and the Thirsty Earth

الْمَطَرُ وَالْأَرْضُ الْعَطْشَى

Two months had passed over the valley village without a drop. The earth cracked like the skin of an old tortoise, the grasses turned yellow, and the dust of the roads got into eyes and bread, and every morning people looked up at the sky. One day, after everyone had prayed and called on God, heavy clouds gathered.

Sindbad, Kalila and Ali arrived at that moment. The rain fell heavily, drumming on the roofs and bouncing off the roads, and the smell of wet earth rose.

Sindbad rejoiced and cried, “The trouble is over!”

But Kalila was watching the ground. The water bounced off the dry, stony soil and ran fast in red threads toward the abandoned riverbed, leaving the fields almost dry.

She said, “Plenty of water, and an earth that does not drink.”

An old farmer said, watching the red threads, “We prayed for this rain for two months, so why is it running away from us?” Kalila said, “It isn’t running away. The soil has hardened like a rusty door, and the water knocks and cannot enter.”

Ali looked at the fields, then at his strong shoulders. He said nothing. He took a pick from the roof of a nearby house, rolled up his sleeves, and began to dig a small trench that cut across the running water and led it to a basin among the trees.

Sindbad followed, carrying two small stones and grumbling at their weight, then the village children, then the men with their axes. They dug small trenches, one after another, and packed stones around the tree trunks to slow the water. Ali said to Sindbad without lifting his head, “Hold this stone here.” Sindbad held it, and found no chance to talk about himself.

And with each trench they cut, the water began to slow, and to sink into the earth, and a warm steam rose from the soil with a sweet smell that no one forgets.

Sindbad stood in mud up to his ankles, having lost one sandal in the mire.

Ali took a stone and set it beside the last trench, and everyone heard the sound of the earth drinking.

Activity

With a grown-up present, take two containers of dry soil. Leave one level and dig small channels in the other, then pour a cup of water over each. Watch which one drinks the water and which lets it run away.

Questions for the guide

  1. Why did the water run over the ground at first and not go into it? What did the people do to hold the rain?
  2. What can you do to receive something that has come to you?

30. The Lantern on the Far Hill

الْقِنْدِيلُ عَلَى التَّلِّ الْبَعِيدِ

On a moonless night, Sindbad climbed to the roof of the high caravanserai, with Kalila and Talia, while the owner of the inn sat by the roof door sipping his tea. The roof was flat and safe, the air cool and lovely, with the smell of woodsmoke from the inn’s hearth.

The stars were so many that he thought someone had scattered salt on a black cloth.

He said, “You know, I feel they are looking at me. All of them.”

Kalila said, “And have you really looked at them? Or were you looking at yourself in a mirror made of stars?”

He did not answer, and scratched his chin like a man caught in the act. Talia raised her hand and said quietly, “There is a star that does not move. And another, near the ground, that trembles.”

Sindbad looked where she pointed. It was a small yellow dot on a far hill, no different from the stars except that it was warmer, and that it shook.

Talia said, “That is not a star. That is a lamp in a house. No smoke reaches a star, and this one shades toward orange.”

The owner said from where he sat, “A shepherd lives there. He lights his lantern every night to see his way to the fold.”

Sindbad went over to the innkeeper and said, “May I borrow your lantern?” The man nodded without setting down his tea, and Sindbad took the small lantern. He raised it above his head and moved it slowly from right to left.

Kalila said in a low voice, “Wait.” A long moment passed, in which they heard a dog barking far away.

And Sindbad heard his own heart beating, and did not know why he longed so much for an answer from a small light.

Then, on the far hill, the yellow dot went out, and was lit twice.

Talia laughed, a muffled laugh, and the innkeeper said, “No one has answered him in years.” And Sindbad stood there with the lantern trembling in his hand, and all the stars around him blinking slowly.

Activity

On a clear evening, go with a grown-up to a flat, safe roof or yard, well away from any edge, and take a small lamp. Switch it off and on twice toward a far-off window, where a friend or relative has agreed beforehand to answer you, and watch what happens.

Questions for the guide

  1. What did Talia discover in the sky, and what did Sindbad do with the lantern?
  2. What do you do when you see a far-off light at night?
