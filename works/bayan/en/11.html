Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive caution. This book grows the courage of apology. Never force a hollow apology, and never humiliate the child. Help him, with empathy, to see the effect of his mistake, so that the apology is sincere. Apologize yourself when you are wrong, so that he learns by example.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings · brief (3 minutes) and full (7 minutes). The tale continues and closes the Branch: Noor has learned dialogue, and now learns to mend what may go wrong in it, by apology.

The Heart of the Book

Noor learned the art of dialogue. But in every dialogue we may err, or wound, or say what we regret. What then? Here is the greatest skill, the crown of the Branch: to admit the mistake and mend it. An apology is not a defeat. It is the courage that rebuilds what broke, and with it the branch bears its first bud of wisdom.

❦  Historical footnote. Salah al-Din al-Ayyubi (d. 589 AH), the forbearing commander, was renowned for pardon in the hour of power, for a gracious apology, and for returning a kindness, mending by pardon what the sword could not. From him we draw that apology and forgiveness are a strength, not a weakness. (In this branch-world he is honoured through Grandmother Ramla's telling, not present in person.)

The Root & Its Kin · a Skill and a Language

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body of apology: an honest gaze into the eyes of the one he wronged, a hand laid on his own heart, and a deed that repairs. He does not parse letters; he apologizes, and feels the broken thing mend. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)
ʿ-dh-rʿudhr · muʿtadhir · maʿdhira · iʿtadhara · maʿdhūr
❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word ʿudhr (apology) has siblings! One who asks forgiveness is a muʿtadhir; the asking itself is a maʿdhira; and when you said ‘I am sorry,’ you iʿtadharta. If the one you wronged accepts your excuse, you are maʿdhūr, excused. One family from one father: ʿ-dh-r.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (ʿ-dh-r) is sound. From it come ʿudhr, iʿtidhār, and maʿdhira. Remind the teacher that a true apology has three parts: admitting the mistake, asking forgiveness, and resolving to mend. A passing word is not enough.

The Full Telling: “Noor's Apology”

For reading aloud with the child · seven minutes · with five original plates in the branch palette

I. Noor errs and wounds her friend, and does not know how to mend

One day, in the heat of a dialogue, Noor said a harsh word to her friend without meaning it: “Your opinion is silly!” Noor regretted it at once, but the word had already flown. Her friend grew sad, and a tear fell, and she turned away.

Noor felt a weight in her chest. Between her and her friend it was as if a pane of glass had cracked. She wanted to mend it, but did not know how. Should she pretend nothing happened? Should she hide? The crack remained, and the sorrow remained.

II. The Stubborn Rock never apologizes, however wrong

And in the garden there was the Stubborn Rock. It erred often, rolling and breaking, blocking the path, yet it never once apologized. Hard, and unyielding. It said to Noor:

“Apology is weakness, Noor! I never say sorry, however wrong I am. Whoever apologizes looks small! Stay hard like me, and never say you're sorry!”

But Noor noticed something: the Stubborn Rock was utterly alone. Everyone it had wounded had drawn away, and it had no friend left. Its stubbornness had not made it strong; it had made it lonely and cold. Whoever does not apologize loses the ones he loves.

III. Grandmother Ramla brings Noor Salah al-Din's wisdom: pardon and apology are strength

And Noor remembered what is told of the forbearing commander Salah al-Din, who pardoned in the hour of power and mended what was broken, and did not disdain to admit and apologize. And Grandmother Ramla was beside her, reminding her of his wisdom; she laid her hand on her heart and said as if carrying his words:

“Noor, do you think an apology is weakness? It is the hardest courage! The weak are stubborn out of fear; the strong admit and mend. When you say ‘I was wrong, and I am sorry,’ you are larger, not smaller.”

Then she added: “A sincere apology is three, Noor: you admit your mistake, you ask forgiveness, and you mend what you broke. And look into her eyes as you apologize, for the heart reaches the heart.”

IV. Noor apologizes sincerely, and the crack is mended with a golden seam

Noor went to her friend, and took a calm breath, and looked into her eyes sincerely, and laid her bough upon her own heart, and said:

“I am sorry, my friend. I was wrong when I said your opinion was silly, it was a harsh word, and untrue. Your opinion matters, and you are dear to me. Please forgive me.”

And a lovely thing happened: the friend's heart softened, her tear dried, and she smiled. The broken glass between them healed; it became stronger, as if a golden thread had mended the crack. A sincere apology did not only repair the friendship; it made it deeper.

V. Reconciled; the Rock softens and apologizes, and a bud grows from a crack

Noor and her friend became closer than before. Noor learned that an apology is a bridge that rebuilds what broke. She said to everyone she had wronged: “I am sorry, forgive me.”, and they loved her more, for an apology is a rare courage.

And the wonder happened: the Stubborn Rock watched Noor, and saw how her friends returned to her. And it tried… and for the first time in its life, it said in a trembling voice: “I… am sorry.” And as it said it, its stone softened a little, and from a crack in it grew a small green bud! It whispered: “I was hard, so I stayed alone; now I have apologized… and I have grown life!”

And the Rock learned that an apology softens the hardest hearts and grows life in them. And so Noor completed the whole journey of the Branch, melody, gesture, dialogue, and apology, and her branch bore its first bud of wisdom, and she was ready for the stage of the Leaves.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Think of a time you wronged someone. How would you say to them: I am sorry, forgive me? Picture their face as they forgive you.”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

The same tale, in shorter sentences.

Noor said a harsh word to her friend without meaning it, and her friend grew sad, and the friendship cracked.

The Stubborn Rock never apologized, so it was left alone; Noor did not copy it.

Noor remembered Salah al-Din's wisdom: “Apology is strength! Admit, ask pardon, and mend what you broke.”

Noor apologized sincerely: “I am sorry, forgive me.” The friendship returned stronger, and the Rock softened and grew a bud.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. A branch that bears a bud (beginning): says “sorry” with a reminder. ← A branch that bears a bud (mastery): admits his mistake unprompted, apologizes sincerely with a gaze, and seeks to mend what he broke. With this the Branch is complete.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR A2 / ACTFL Intermediate Low). The child can offer a threefold sincere apology, admitting the mistake, asking forgiveness, and offering to mend, with an honest gaze, to repair a relationship he has hurt.

CASEL Behavioural Criteria (Responsible Decision-Making & Self-Awareness)

  • Admits his mistake without stubbornness.
  • Apologizes sincerely and looks into the eyes of the one he wronged.
  • Seeks to mend what he broke.
  • Forgives another who apologizes to him.

The Four Inner Senses of the Apology

An apology, too, is felt in the body before it is a skill. CRT awakens the child's interoception of repair. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in apologizingA guiding whisper
The weight (remorse)The heaviness in the chest after a wrong“Feel the weight in your chest; it is asking you to mend.”
The courage to admitThe brave, warm effort of saying “I was wrong”“Feel your brave heart as you say: I was wrong.”
The mending (the golden seam)The relief as the broken bond heals stronger“Feel the crack close, warm and gold, stronger than before.”
The lightness (after)The lifted weight once you are forgiven“Feel your chest grow light when the weight is set down.”
❦  How to use these whispers. Offer them after a real but small mishap, once the heat has passed, in the order shown. Let the child point to where in the body each one sits before he says a word.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. The child is not forced into a hollow, insincere apology, for a forced apology loses its meaning. We first help him, with empathy, to understand the effect of his mistake, so the apology comes from his heart. And we do not humiliate him before others.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Autism spectrum (ASD)May not grasp the effect of his words on the other; explain with clear Social Stories, and teach a simple fixed apology form.
Emotional-expression difficultyTeach the apology sentence as a form, linked to a picture of the other's feeling so he understands the effect.
Deep shynessA written apology or a gesture (offering a small gift, a drawing) is accepted if the direct word is hard.
Pride / defensivenessTeach that apology is courage, not defeat, by example: adults apologize before him when they err.
An environment that ties apology to punishmentReassure the child that an apology is to repair the bond, not to bring punishment, so he does not fear to admit.
Cultural differenceRespect differing apology forms across cultures; what matters is sincere remorse and the intent to mend.

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the screen-box. Put devices away. Screens are full of stubbornness and pride; here we teach the courage to admit a fault, which they rarely show.

ii. During the session · the bridge of forgiveness. Act out a small mistake, and train the child in the threefold apology: I was wrong, I am sorry, I will mend. Then forgiveness, with a hug.

iii. After the session · the grown-ups' apology. Apologize yourself to the child when you wrong him. When he sees a grown-up apologize, he learns that an apology is strength, not shame.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many times did our child apologize sincerely on his own, or forgive another?” A star for each. Count the stars at week's end.

“The screen teaches stubbornness. We teach the courage of apology.”

ʿUdhr, muʿtadhir and maʿdhira share the root ʿ-dh-r. Musāmaḥa (from s-m-ḥ) and nadam (from n-d-m) come from other roots. They sit here because an apology needs both: remorse before it, and forgiveness after.

Output (Arabic): «أَعْتَذِرُ بِصِدْقٍ، فَأُصْلِحُ مَا كَسَرْتُ، وَأَكْبُرُ.»
English Bridge: “I apologize, so I mend what I broke.” Saying sorry is not weakness; it is the strength that heals.

Questions of Understanding

  • What happened to the friendship when Noor said the harsh word? recall
  • Why was the Stubborn Rock left alone, despite its strength? inference
  • Imagine you have wronged a friend. How would you apologize to him sincerely, now? sensory
  • Have you ever felt regret and fear at once, before apologizing? simultaneous emotions
  • The Rock was stubborn because it feared looking small. Have you ever refused to apologize out of that fear? What could help the Rock feel safe enough to say sorry? empathy for the other
  • When did someone apologize to you sincerely? How did your feeling toward him change? personal link
An apology is strength, not weakness; whoever admits his mistake mends it and grows larger.

❦  Behavioural translation (not a slogan only). Teach the child: “When you wrong someone, look into his eyes and say: I am sorry, I was wrong, forgive me. Then mend what you broke. An apology heals hearts.” By apology, Noor mended her friendship.

Craft & Media

The drawing. Ask the child to make a “forgiveness card” for someone he wronged: he draws a broken heart healed with a golden thread, and writes a word of apology.

The play. Two children act a scene: one errs (breaks a toy, say) and practises the threefold apology (I was wrong, I am sorry, I will mend), and the other practises forgiveness. Then they switch.

Listening. There is no recording of this book yet. Read the tale aloud, slowly, and keep the pauses. The Listening room gathers the recordings that exist.

Covenant & Publication Statements

What this book promises, beyond the standards of the series:

  • The root ʿ-dh-r is sound. The child meets it through the body of apology (the steady gaze, the hand on the heart, the repairing deed); the morphology stays with the teacher.
  • Salah al-Din is invoked only through Grandmother Ramla's telling and is never present in person, and the Stubborn Rock is marked as symbolic, so history and symbol stay apart.
  • No apology is forced and no child is humiliated. The Rock has a believable motive, the fear of looking small, and is met with an empathy question rather than a verdict.
  • The closing echo looks back to dialogue and forward to empathy: this book completes the Branch and opens the Leaves.

The Branch's Apology

To free the world's children from the epidemic of screens, and teach them the rare courage of a sincere apology.

Noor has mastered dialogue, but in the heat of a talk she wounds her friend with a harsh word, and a crack opens between them. When the Stubborn Rock tells her that apology is weakness, the remembered wisdom of a forbearing commander, passed on by her grandmother, teaches her that pardon and apology are strength, and that a true apology has three parts: admit, ask pardon, and mend. And when at last she looks into her friend's eyes and says “I am sorry,” the broken bond heals along a golden seam, stronger than before, and even the hard rock softens and grows a green bud. So the Branch is complete, and bears its first bud of wisdom. A tangible, sensory tale in the tradition of Concrete Reading Theory: no stubborn screen · only the brave, mending word of a child.
