Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive caution. This book grows storytelling. Remember the child may need time to order his thoughts, do not interrupt him or finish for him; grant him the silence of thinking. Every child is a storyteller by nature, if given the time.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings · brief (3 minutes) and full (7 minutes). The tale is the close of the Bud: Noor gathers her skills, and learns to tell an ordered story with her hands, so her bud completes itself, ready for the branch.

The Heart of the Book

Noor learned to listen, to ask, and to be gentle. Now, at the close of the Bud, she learns to gather all of it into a story: to order her words so they have a beginning, a middle, and an end. An ordered story arrives; scattered words are lost. And so Noor's bud completes itself: she no longer waits for someone to tell her a story, she tells the world what stirs within her.

❦  Historical footnote. Abdullah ibn al-Muqaffa' (d. c. 139 AH) was a great Arab man of letters of the second Hijri century, who rendered the tales of Kalila wa Dimna on the tongues of animals, teaching wisdom through story. From him we draw the art of the ordered tale that carries meaning.

The Root & Its Kin · a Skill and a Language

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body of telling– three ordering steps counted on three fingers, hands moving to shape the tale. He does not parse letters; he tells, and feels the story fall into order. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)
ḥ – k – yḥikāya · ḥākin · maḥkiyy · ḥakā · yaḥkī
❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word ḥikāya (story) has siblings! One who tells the story is a ḥākin (a narrator); the tale that was told is maḥkiyy; when you order your story and tell it you say ḥakaytu, and you do it each day so you taḥkī. One family from one father: ḥ-k-y.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (ḥ-k-y) is defective (its final radical is a weak letter, the yā'). The yā' shows in “yaḥkī” and turns to an alif in “ḥakā”, a natural variation of the defective root, noted for the teacher.

The Full Telling: “Noor's Story”

For reading aloud with the child · seven minutes · with five original plates in the bud palette

I. Noor wants to tell what happened, but her words scatter out of order

Noor could listen, and ask, and speak gently. And one day, something lovely happened to her that she wanted to tell her friends. But she began from the middle, then jumped to the end, then went back to the beginning…

So her friends understood nothing! They looked at her, puzzled. The words were all correct, but scattered and out of order, and the story was lost like leaves the wind had thrown about.

II. The Scattered Whirlwind leaps from tale to tale with no beginning or end

That day the Scattered Whirlwind blew in. It told a thousand tales at once, leaping from one to another without finishing any:

“And there was a lion, no, wait, and in the sea a fish, (panting), and then the bird flew, where were we? Never mind! And there's a castle, quick, another story!”

It was panting from all its leaping, for scattered speech tires its own teller too. Noor tried the Whirlwind's way: she told everything at once, with no order. Her friends grew dizzy, and drew away. A scattered story is understood by no one, however exciting.

III. Ibn al-Muqaffa' and Grandmother Ramla teach Noor the secret of the ordered story

Beside the soil sat the man of letters Abdullah ibn al-Muqaffa', who told the loveliest tales on the tongues of animals. And with him Grandmother Ramla, whom we know from before.

Ibn al-Muqaffa' said, moving his hands: “Noor, every lovely story has three steps, like the fingers of your hand. First, the beginning, who, and where. Second, the middle, what happened. Third, the end, how it all turned out.”

And Grandmother Ramla added: “And use your hands, Noor, as you tell, the hands help the words fall into order. One story, from its beginning to its end, is lovelier than a thousand scattered ones.”

IV. Noor tells an ordered story using her hands (her two leaves)

Noor took a calm breath, and ordered her story in her head first, then began to tell, using her two leaves like hands that point:

“In the beginning: I was in the garden in the morning. Then: I found a lost butterfly and helped it find its flower. And in the end: the butterfly flew away happy, and thanked me!”

It was a lovely, ordered story: a beginning, a middle, and an end. So everyone understood it, and lived it with her, and rejoiced in her joy. Noor felt an overflowing happiness, her story had arrived!

V. Noor ends her story to everyone's delight; the Whirlwind calms and tells in order; the bud nearly a branch

Noor's friends clapped for her ordered story. And with each ordered tale she told, her bud grew more, until it nearly put out two small branches, she was on the threshold of becoming a branch!

And the wonder happened: the Scattered Whirlwind calmed, and stopped spinning, and told, for the first time, one whole story: a beginning, a middle, and an end. It whispered in wonder: “I used to tell a thousand tales and no one understood me. Now I told one ordered tale… and everyone listened.”

And the Whirlwind learned that one ordered story is more eloquent than a thousand scattered ones. And so Noor completed the whole journey of the Bud: she listened, and asked, and was gentle, and told, and she was ready to become a strong branch.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Think of something that happened to you today. Order it in three: how did it begin? what happened? how did it end? Now tell it with your hands.”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

Short sentences, the selfsame meaning.

Noor wanted to tell what happened to her, but she scattered her words, and no one understood.

The Scattered Whirlwind blew in, leaping between a thousand tales with no order, and everyone grew dizzy.

Ibn al-Muqaffa' said: “Every story has three steps: a beginning, a middle, and an end. And use your hands.”

Noor told an ordered story: “In the beginning… then… and in the end.” Everyone understood, and the Whirlwind calmed and told in order.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. A completed bud (beginning)– tells a story in two steps (a beginning and an end) with help. ← A completed bud (mastery)– tells a short story in three ordered steps using his hands, unaided. With this the bud completes itself.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR A1 / ACTFL Novice Mid). The child can tell a short personal story in a clear order, a beginning, a middle, and an end, using his hands to help, so a listener follows the sequence and understands.

CASEL Behavioral Criteria (Relationship & Expression Skills)

  • Orders his story into a beginning, a middle, and an end.
  • Uses his hands and face to illustrate.
  • Tells one connected story without scattered leaping.
  • Links the parts of his story with words (then, after that, finally).

The Four Inner Senses of the Story

A story, too, is felt in the body before it is told. CRT awakens the child's interoception of order and shape. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in tellingA guiding whisper
The ordering (before speaking)The quiet settling of events into first, then, last“Line up your three pictures in your mind before you speak.”
The three-count of the handThree fingers, three steps · the shape felt in the hand“Feel the beginning, middle, and end on three fingers.”
The thread of sequenceThe pull that carries each part into the next“Feel each part hand the next one along.”
The arrivalThe warm relief when a listener follows and understands“Did you feel your story land in your friend's eyes?”
❦  Why this matters. A child who feels the ordering, the three-count, the thread, and the arrival can shape any experience into a story that carries, the gathering of every earlier skill into one whole.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. The child is not forced to tell before everyone if he is shy. We graduate it gently: he tells first in a whisper to himself, then to a doll, then to one companion, then to the group when he is at ease. The story is a joy, not an exam, and its details are not corrected harshly while he tells.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Narrative-language difficultyUse three picture cards (beginning, middle, end) that he orders and tells about in a few words.
Autism spectrum (ASD)Teach the story's shape visually (a story map), and accept his favourite subjects however specialised.
Shyness / anxietyHe tells first in a whisper or to a doll, then to a companion, before the group, no pressure.
Hyperactivity / attentionShorten the story to three sentences, and allow movement and hand-gestures while telling.
Speech difficultyTelling by gesture, ordering pictures, or mime is accepted instead of full speech.
Cultural differenceRespect differing storytelling traditions; some cultures open a tale with a proverb or refrain before ordering its beginning, middle, and end.

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the screen-box. Put devices away. The screen tells the child stories; we want him to tell · for the child who tells thinks, orders, and creates.

ii. During the session · the three-finger story. Help the child tell in three steps on his fingers: beginning (a finger), middle (a finger), end (a finger). Make it a game.

iii. After the session · the story of the day. Before sleep, ask the child to tell his day in three ordered steps. A habit that builds memory and order.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many ordered stories (beginning-middle-end) did our child tell?” A star for each. Count the stars at week's end.

“The screen tells the child stories. We make the child the storyteller.”

The Juthur Scale (JPS) · Book Seven Unit

❦  How the measure grows. With this book the Bud stage completes (listening, question, gentleness, story). The new dimension here is ordered narrative expression, in preparation for the Branch.

Filled twice, by two independent raters where possible. The marks are behaviorally anchored:

Questions of Understanding

  • Why did Noor's friends not understand her story at first? recall
  • Why did those who heard the Scattered Whirlwind grow dizzy? inference
  • Tell with your hands something that happened to you, in three steps, did you feel the difference? sensory
  • Have you ever felt excited and muddled at once, trying to tell too much at the same time? simultaneous emotions
  • The Whirlwind rushed a thousand tales because it feared being unheard. Have you ever piled on words to be interesting? What could help the Whirlwind tell just one whole story? empathy for the other
  • What is the loveliest story you have heard? How did it begin and end? personal link
My story begins, then moves, then ends; ordered words are a bridge, and scattered ones a fog.

❦  Behavioral translation (not a slogan only). Teach the child: “When you tell something that happened, begin with its start, then what happened, then how it ended. Three steps: beginning, middle, end.” By ordering, people understood Noor's story.

The Twelve Zero-Standards

The covenant of “nothing missing, nothing forced”, twelve zeroes the book must hold.

  • Zero logical error– the tale runs scattered words → dizzy chaos → the ordered story → arrival, unbroken.
  • Zero moral coercion– no child is forced to tell before all; the shy are graduated gently.
  • Zero structural contradiction– the two sub-degrees close the Bud arc consistently.
  • Zero psychological gap– Noor feels muddled, then overflowing joy; the emotion fits the age.
  • Zero disconnected episode– the bridge gathers the whole Bud and opens the Branch.
  • Zero passive activity– the story becomes three fingers, two hands, ordered speech.
  • Zero stock icon– the seal and all five plates are original vector art.
  • Zero symbolic error– the completed bud tells its journey and nearly branches; the metaphor holds.
  • Zero psychological miscue– the Whirlwind learns to order; it is understood, not defeated.
  • Zero architectural break– identity → root → tale → application → measure → review is whole.
  • Zero cognitive coercion– “one ordered story beats a thousand scattered ones” is discovery, not dictation.
  • Zero cultural imbalance– a male man of letters (ibn al-Muqaffa') and a female elder (Ramla) share the wisdom.
