Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive caution. This book grows turn-taking in dialogue. An impulsive child may interrupt often: remind him gently with the “talking ball,” not with a scolding. And give the shy child time to take his turn without embarrassment.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings: a brief telling (3 minutes) and a full telling (7 minutes). The tale continues: Noor the branch expresses beautifully, and now learns to make her speech an exchange, not a monopoly.

The Heart of the Book

Noor learned the skills of expression: voice, melody, and body. But all of it was speech from one side. Now she learns the art that crowns them: dialogue, to take her turn and give the other his. Speech alone is a sermon; dialogue is the meeting of two minds.

❦  Historical footnote. Ibn Rushd (d. 595 AH), a philosopher, judge, and physician of Córdoba, was renowned for reasoned dialogue, sound inference, and fairness to those who disagreed. From him we draw the manners of dialogue: to exchange views by argument, not to overpower. (In this branch-world he is honoured through Grandmother Ramla's telling, not present in person, to keep the line between history and symbol clear.) What Grandmother Ramla says of his manners is the author's own composition, not a text from him; she is a fictional character.

The Root & Its Kin · a Skill and a Language

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body of turn-taking: speaking, then a held pause, then a turn handed over like a ball. He does not parse letters; he converses, and feels speech go and return. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)
ḥ-w-rḥiwār · muḥāwir · muḥāwara · taḥāwara · mutaḥāwirūn
❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word ḥiwār (dialogue) has siblings! One who talks with you and trades views is a muḥāwir; the sitting where you exchange speech is a muḥāwara; and when you two talked by turns you taḥāwartumā. One family from one father: ḥ-w-r.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (ḥ-w-r) is hollow (its middle radical is a wāw). From it come ḥiwār and muḥāwara, the trading of speech between two. The root's older sense is “to return”: a dialogue is speech that returns between two parties, back and forth.

The Full Telling: “Noor's Dialogue”

For reading aloud with the child · seven minutes · with five original plates in the branch palette

I. Noor speaks well, but never stops and lets no one else speak

Noor came to speak with mastery: a tuned voice, an expressive body, well-ordered tales. But she fell into a new mistake: she talked, and talked, and talked, without stopping! She let no one say a word.

Beside her was a small shrub that wished to share its view, but Noor never paused. “I saw… and I know… and I also…” So the shrub shut its mouth and grew sad, and felt as if it did not exist. Speech from one side is not a dialogue.

II. The Sweeping River floods on without stopping, and no one can speak

That day the Sweeping River ran. It surged without stopping, filling every space with its voice, so no one found a place to speak. Whoever came near was flooded by its flow. It ran so because it feared that if it paused, someone else would take its turn. It said to Noor as it ran:

“Never stop, Noor! Flow like me, without a break! Whoever pauses loses his turn. Fill every silence with your speech, and leave no room for anyone!”

Noor tried the River's way: she talked without pause. But everyone drew away, for no one likes to be only a listener all the time. She was left alone, talking to herself. She discovered that whoever does not stop to listen is left alone.

III. Grandmother Ramla teaches Noor the manners of dialogue, in the spirit of Ibn Rushd

Grandmother Ramla was beside her, and she told Noor of the wise Ibn Rushd, the philosopher of Córdoba, who is remembered for answering those who differed from him with argument and fairness, listening first and replying after. Then she said, in the spirit of his way:

“Noor, dialogue is like a ball: you throw it, then wait for it to be returned. You speak, then you stop and ask: and what do you think? Then you listen for the answer. So two minds meet, and each learns from the other.”

And Grandmother Ramla added: “You are a branch, Noor, and beside you are branches. Let your leaves sway, then stop so another's leaves may sway. The silence after your speech is a gift you give the one who talks with you.”

IV. Noor speaks, then stops and gives the turn, and they exchange speech

Noor tried. She said her view in a sentence, then (and this was the new thing) she stopped, and looked at the shrub, and asked: “This is my view… and what is yours?”

The shrub rejoiced, and opened its mouth at last, and gave its view. Then Noor listened, then replied, then listened again… And a lovely thing happened: a dialogue was born! Speech going and returning, like a ball between two friends. Noor learned from the shrub things she had not known, and the shrub learned from her. Speech became a meeting, not a monopoly.

V. A lovely mutual dialogue; the River learns to pause and becomes two rivers taking turns

Noor came to dialogue with everyone: she spoke, then listened; asked, then waited. She said to her friend with love: “This is what I see… and what do you see?” And everyone loved to talk with her, because she gave them a place to speak.

The Sweeping River had been watching Noor, and now it tried… and for the first time it paused a little! When it paused, its course split into two small rivers that took turns gently. It said, in a quieter voice: “I ran alone, so I drowned everyone; now I have stopped to listen… and we run together!”

And the River learned that a dialogue is two rivers meeting, not one that swallows the other. Noor kept speaking, then listening, and her Branch skills were nearly complete; she was close to bearing her first bud of wisdom.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Say one sentence to the one beside you, then stop, and ask: and what do you think? Then listen. So a dialogue begins.”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

Short sentences, the same meaning.

Noor came to speak with mastery, but she never stopped and let no one speak, so the shrub grew sad.

The Sweeping River ran without stopping, flooding all; Noor copied it, and everyone drew away.

Grandmother Ramla reminded Noor of Ibn Rushd's way: “Dialogue is a ball. You speak, then stop and ask, ‘And what do you think?’”

Noor spoke, then stopped and asked: “And what do you think?” A dialogue was born! And the River learned to pause.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. A responsive branch (beginning): stops after speaking to listen, with a reminder. ← A responsive branch (mastery): speaks, then stops unprompted, asks the other for his view, and builds on his reply in a mutual exchange.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR A2 / ACTFL Intermediate Low). The child can hold a short two-way exchange: state a view, hand over the turn with a question, listen, and build on the answer, so speech becomes a shared conversation.

CASEL Behavioral Criteria (Relationship Skills & Responsible Decision-Making)

  • Speaks, then stops to let the other speak.
  • Asks the other for his view and listens to his answer.
  • Builds on the other's words and does not leap over them.
  • Keeps a balance of give-and-take in the talk.

The Four Inner Senses of Dialogue

Dialogue, too, is felt in the body before it is a skill. CRT awakens the child's interoception of exchange. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in dialogueA guiding whisper
The handover (the pause)The small, generous stop that hands the turn over“Feel the pause where you give your friend the turn.”
The returnThe pleasure of a word coming back, like a ball“Feel the words come back to you, and back again.”
Building-onThe joining of your idea onto another's“Feel your thought lean on your friend's, and grow.”
The balance (give and take)The even weight of speaking and listening“Are you both carrying the talk, side by side?”
❦  Why this matters. A child who feels the handover, the return, the building-on, and the balance can turn any talk into a true meeting, and will never be left alone, talking to himself.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. If the child rushes on without stopping, remind him gently with the ball-game (your turn, then his), not with a scolding. And if he is shy and does not take his turn, encourage him gently and do not embarrass him.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Hyperactivity / impulsivityUse a physical object (the talking ball): only its holder speaks; this orders the turns tangibly.
Autism spectrum (ASD)Teach turn-taking signals explicitly (now it's your turn) and use a visual dialogue schedule.
Shyness / anxietyBegin dialogue in a calm, familiar pair, with enough time to take a turn, without pressure.
Language difficultyA turn taken with a short sentence or a gesture is accepted; the exchange matters, not fluency.
Hearing impairmentMake sure the child heard the other's view (by watching the face or in writing) before replying.
Cultural differenceRespect differing dialogue norms in the rhythm of turns and the silences between them.

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the screen-box. Put devices away. The screen talks and awaits no reply; we teach the child the art of exchange the screen does not know.

ii. During the session · the talking-ball game. Use a small ball: its holder says one sentence, then passes it on for a reply. Dialogue becomes a felt exchange.

iii. After the session · the dinner dialogue. At the table, each speaks in turn and asks another: and what do you think? No one monopolizes the talk.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many times did our child ask another for his view and listen?” A star for each. Count the stars at week's end.

Questions of Understanding

  • Why did the shrub beside Noor grow sad at first? recall
  • Why was the Sweeping River left alone, though it spoke so much? inference
  • Say a sentence to the one beside you, then ask, “And what do you think?” What happened? sensory
  • Have you ever felt eager to speak and curious to hear, at once, in a talk? simultaneous emotions
  • The River filled every silence because it feared to lose its turn. Have you ever talked over others out of that fear? What could help the River pause and share? empathy for the other
  • When did you feel someone truly dialogued with you, and gave you your turn? personal link
Dialogue is give-and-take. I speak and you listen, you speak and I listen, and so we understand together.

❦  Behavioral translation (not a slogan only). Teach the child: “When you speak, say your sentence, then stop and ask, ‘And what do you think?’ A dialogue is a ball we toss to each other, not one that a single person keeps.” By mutual dialogue, Noor understood others, and they loved her.

Craft & Media

The drawing. Ask the child to draw two neighbouring branches, with two arrows between them (one going, one returning): the speech that goes and comes back in a dialogue.

The play. Two children play “building the story by turns”: one says a sentence of a tale, the other builds the next, and so on. No one finishes the story alone.

Listening. There is no recording of this book yet. Read the tale aloud, slowly, and keep the pauses. The Listening room gathers the recordings that exist.

The Branch's Dialogue

To give children a calm place beside the screens, and teach the child the art of exchange the screen does not know.

Noor can express with voice, melody, and body, but all of it is speech from one side, and she never stops to let another speak. When the Sweeping River urges her to fill every silence, a grandmother, recalling the way of a great philosopher, teaches her that dialogue is a ball you throw and await, and to speak, then stop, and ask, “And what do you think?” And when at last speech goes and returns like a ball between friends, a real dialogue is born, and even the flooding river learns to pause and split into two streams that share. A tangible, sensory tale in the tradition of Concrete Reading Theory: no screen that talks and never listens, only two minds, meeting.
