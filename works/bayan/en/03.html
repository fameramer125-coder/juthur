This book closes the journey of the Seed: after the breath and the voice, Noor learns to see the one she addresses, and her shell splits open, ready to become a bud. The Single Thread · the cord binding the twenty books is Presence. The Breath was presence of the self; the Voice, presence made audible. This book is presence turned toward the other: the gaze, the first silent bridge between two selves, and the close of the Seed. It reaches back to breath and voice, and forward to true listening.

Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive educational caution. This book trains looking into the eyes. For some children (especially on the autism spectrum) direct eye contact can be distressing. Never force a child to look into the eyes; a look toward the forehead or the nose suffices. The aim is to turn toward the other, not to stare.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings: a brief telling (3 minutes) and a full telling (7 minutes). The tale is the close of the Seed's journey: after breath and voice, Noor learns to see the one she addresses, so her shell splits and she is ready to become a bud.

The Heart of the Book

After the breath and the voice comes the first real bridge toward the other: the gaze. To see the one you address before you speak, to see yourself reflected in his eye, and to be seen. This is the first silent dialogue; and by it the seed's shell splits, and it is ready to grow.

❦  Historical footnote. The Wise Poet in the story is a fictional figure in whom we draw on insight (baṣīra, from the root b-ṣ-r, close in sense to n-ẓ-r) as it shone in the art of the poets, among them Hassan ibn Thabit al-Ansari, may God be pleased with him, the poet of the Prophet ﷺ. We do not depict the Companions as characters in the tales, out of respect for their standing. Insight is to see the person and the meaning deeply before you speak, for the honest word begins with an honest gaze. What the poet and Grandmother Ramla say in the story is the author's own composition.

The Root & Its Kin · a Skill and a Language

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body's eyes, never through analysis. He does not parse letters; he looks, and discovers the small living reflection of himself in another's eye. The anchor is a felt, relational seeing. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)

❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word naẓar (looking) has siblings! A quick look is a naẓra; one who looks is a nāẓir; a beautiful sight is a manẓar; and the instrument that brings the far near is a minẓār (a telescope). One family from one father: n-ẓ-r.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (n-ẓ-r) is sound (no weak letter). From it come both seeing with the eye (naẓar) and reflecting with the mind (naẓara fī l-amr, to consider a matter): two meanings from one origin.

The Full Telling: “Noor's Gaze”

For reading aloud with the child (ages 5–6) · seven minutes · with five original plates in the seed palette

I. Noor breathes and has a voice, but she speaks with her eyes far away

Noor had learned to breathe, and to make her breath a voice. She spoke a great deal now! But she spoke with her eyes cast to the ground, or to some far place, never at the one she was speaking to.

She said much to the rain, but she did not look at it. She felt her words getting lost in the air, never arriving. And all the while her little shell had begun to crack, just a little…

II. The Wandering Mist drifts, seeing no one, and Noor copies it

That day the Wandering Mist drifted by. It swept along everywhere, its eyes vacant, looking at no one, speaking to everyone and to no one at once. It said to Noor in a foggy voice:

“Looking is a waste of time, Noor! Just talk, and keep your eyes to yourself. I look at no one, and I am everywhere!”

Noor tried the Mist's way: she talked and talked without looking. But her words kept scattering, reaching no one's heart. She felt a strange loneliness, as if she were speaking from behind a pane of misty glass.

III. The Wise Poet and Grandmother Ramla teach Noor the secret of the gaze, and a dewdrop reflects

Beside the soil sat the Wise Poet, the man who sees the meaning before he says it. And with him Grandmother Ramla, whom we know from before.

Grandmother Ramla said tenderly: “Noor, speech without a gaze is like rain without earth: it falls and grows nothing. Look at me now, into my eyes, just three seconds. What do you see?”

Noor looked shyly into Grandmother's eyes… then her own eyes widened in wonder: “I see… I see my own small self in your eyes, Grandmother! A tiny picture of me lives there!”

Grandmother smiled: “Some children see their picture, some see the colour of the eye or its shine, and all of it is beautiful. What matters is not what you see, but that you see your friend, and that he feels you see him.”

The poet smiled and said: “That is the secret, Noor. When you look into the eye of the one you speak to, you see yourself in him, and he sees himself in you. You come to be together. This is the first dialogue, before any word.”

IV. Noor lifts her eyes and looks clearly, and light enters through her cracking shell

Noor lifted her eyes, those eyes that had looked far away, and looked straight ahead. And when she looked, a wondrous thing happened: her shell split further, and the light came in through the crack. She began to see more clearly, and to feel that she was… larger.

For the first time, Noor was no longer alone behind the glass. Between her and the world there was now a bridge, a bridge made of a gaze.

V. Noor looks and speaks, and her words arrive; the Mist clears and sees for the first time

Noor looked at the rain for three seconds, then said in the voice she had learned: “I see you.” And this time her word arrived, because it came out of an honest gaze.

Then the Wandering Mist did what it had never done: it stopped drifting for a moment… and tried. For the first time it looked, truly looked at Noor, and saw her. It whispered: “I… I was everywhere, but I never saw anyone at all. Now I see you… and I see that I was lonely.” And the Mist began to learn, not all at once, but little by little, that whoever does not look neither sees nor is seen; and it knew it would need time and practice, as Noor had once needed.

Then Noor looked at her split shell, and knew that the journey of the Seed was complete, and that she was ready to become a bud.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Look at the one beside you, three seconds. Did you see your small picture in his eye? Now, speak.”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

For a child of four · short sentences, the selfsame meaning.

Noor had learned the breath and the voice. But she spoke with her eyes far away, so her words did not arrive.

The Wandering Mist passed and said, “Look at no one!” So Noor copied it, and felt lonely.

Grandmother Ramla said, “Look into my eyes, three seconds.” Noor looked… and saw her own small picture in the eye!

Noor looked at the rain and said, “I see you!” Her word arrived, and the Mist cleared and saw. And Noor's shell split: she was ready to become a bud.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. A cracking seed (beginning): looks toward the other a moment with a reminder. ← A cracking seed (mastery): turns toward the one he addresses and looks at him before he speaks, unprompted (a reassured look, not a fixed stare). With this the Seed stage is complete, and he is ready for the Bud.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR pre-A1 / ACTFL Novice Low). The child can turn toward a partner and hold a brief, warm gaze to open a conversation, sending the message “I see you, I am with you” before his first words, and as the ground of them.

CASEL Behavioral Criteria (Self-Awareness & the seed of Social Awareness)

  • Looks toward the one he addresses before he begins to speak.
  • Describes that he “saw” the other (e.g., I saw his eyes, I saw his smile).
  • Realizes that the gaze is a two-way exchange (he sees me and I see him).
  • Turns toward the other and looks, without turning his face away or covering his eyes.

The Four Inner Senses of the Gaze

The gaze, too, is felt before it is understood. CRT awakens the child's interoception of connection. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in the gazeA guiding whisper
The turning (orienting)The gentle turn of the whole self toward another“Turn your face and your heart toward your friend.”
The reflection (being seen)Seeing his own small self living in another's eye, and being seen back“Can you find your tiny picture in your friend's eye?”
WarmthThe soft warmth of a met, welcomed gaze“Does a kind look feel warm, like sunlight?”
Balance (glance, not stare)A look that is neither a hard stare nor an escape“Look softly, then let your eyes rest: a look, not a stare.”
❦  Why this matters. A child who feels the turning, the reflection, the warmth, and the balance of a gaze owns the first tool of every dialogue: to be present with another, and not merely near.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. If the child feels uneasy or tense from direct looking, stop at once. Looking into the eyes is an invitation, not an obligation, and a long stare is not the goal, but turning toward the other with affection is.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Autism spectrum (ASD)Eye contact is never imposed; a look toward the forehead, nose, or shoulder suffices. Turning toward the other is the aim, not staring into the eyes.
Visual impairmentReplace looking with turning the face toward the source of the voice, or a gentle touch on the hand that says: I am attending to you.
Social anxietyBegin with a doll or a picture, then one familiar companion, before any group. Start with a fleeting glance (one second), then two, then three; each look is enough.
Hyperactivity / attentionTie the gaze to a fun game: how many colours can you see in your friend's eye? Keep it to a short spell.
Sensory sensitivityNo long staring; a fleeting glance suffices and grows gradually with the child's comfort.
Cultural differenceIn some cultures direct eye contact with elders is impolite. This is respected; a look between peers, or a courteous turning, suffices.

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the Gaze-Box. This week, give the device box a new name: the Gaze-Box! The screen pulls the gaze away from faces and keeps the child's eyes busy and away from the eyes of those who love him. Put the devices in, and return the child's gaze to the eyes.

ii. During the session · the mirror in the eye. Sit facing the child and say, “Look into my eye. Can you see your small self there?” Most children are amazed at the reflection. Make the gaze a warm game, never a duty.

iii. After the session · three seconds before speaking. Train the habit gently: before the child asks for something, he looks into the eyes three seconds, then speaks. Notice how the arrival of his words changes.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many times did our child look into the eyes before speaking today?” Give a star for each time he does it himself. Count the stars at week's end.

“The screen teaches the child to look at glass. We teach him to look at a human being.”

Questions of Understanding

  • Why did Noor's words get lost at first and never arrive? recall
  • Why did the Wandering Mist feel lonely, though it was everywhere? inference
  • Look into the eyes of the one beside you for three seconds. What did you see in them? sensory
  • Have you ever felt shy and curious at the same time when you looked into someone's eyes? simultaneous emotions
  • The Wandering Mist looked at no one, and so felt alone. Have you ever felt unseen? What could help the Mist learn to see others, gently, a little at a time? empathy for the other
  • When do you feel your words have truly “arrived” at the other? personal link
Before I speak, I look; for an honest gaze is the first bridge between me and you.

❦  Behavioral translation (not a slogan only). Teach the child the rule: “Before you ask for something or say something important, look into the eyes first, then speak.” Noor's words arrived only when a gaze went before them.

Craft & Media

The drawing. Ask the child to draw a large eye, and in its centre a small picture of the person he loves to see. Who lives in your eye?

The play. Two children (or a child with a doll or a mirror): one is the Wandering Mist who speaks without looking, the other asks him to look. Then they trade. How does the feeling change when one truly looks at the other?

Listening. There is no recording of this book yet. Read the tale aloud, slowly, and keep the pauses. The Listening room gathers the recordings that exist.

The Seed's Gaze

To give children a calm place beside the screens, and return them to the body, the breath, the voice, and the living gaze.

Little Noor can breathe and speak now, but her words keep getting lost, because her eyes look away. When the Wandering Mist tells her to look at no one, a wise poet and a grandmother teach her, by a gaze of three seconds, to find her own small reflection in a friend's eye. And when at last she looks and says “I see you,” her word arrives, the Mist clears, and her shell splits open: the Seed is ready to become a Bud. A tangible, sensory tale in the tradition of Concrete Reading Theory: no screen, no drill, only two selves, and the first bridge of a gaze.
