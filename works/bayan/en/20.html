The Single Thread · where the thread becomes a circle. Every book was one skill of presence; now the last root, the hidden root, is revealed as the secret of them all: presence of the heart, and the passing of the seed to the one who comes after. The ripe fruit is not an end: it carries a seed, and the circle turns back to the first breath. Presence is the first manner of dialogue, and all expression begins with our being truly here.

Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive caution. This closing book is a mirror and a hope, not a trial. Its aim is a tender wakefulness, not blame, and it does not demonize technology, which is a useful tool; the trouble comes only when it takes the present away. Let no learner feel that his family does not love him, and lay no guilt on a tired mother or father. Open hearts gently. And if a child truly lives with neglect, surround him with warmth and guide him to a grown-up he trusts. Let every parent be reassured: you are not alone in this. Awareness is a step of love, not a condemnation, and a small beginning is enough.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings · brief (3 minutes) and full (7 minutes). This time the tale is real: a dinner table in a home like any home, crowning all we learned of expression and revealing its hidden secret: the root. Here Noor (the symbol of the journey) meets the real world in a child's drawing. A book to be read by the whole family together.

The Heart of the Book

Noor has come, across twenty books, from a small seed to a fruiting tree. And now, at the close, we discover that all we learned of expression (the breath, the voice, the listening, the dialogue, the empathy, the silence, the counsel) could never have borne fruit but for a hidden root that works in silence. And the fruit, when it ripens, is not an end but carries in its heart a new seed, and so the circle begins again. This is legacy: not to give the fruit, but to give the seed that renews life.

❦  A note on the sources of wisdom. In this closing book the wisdom comes from two sources near to every child: a teacher who poses a question that opens the mind, and a grandmother, Ramla, who has walked with us since the seed, present and silent at the table. From them we draw that the greatest wisdom may come from the smallest mouth: from a child who sees what we do not see.

The Root & Its Kin · the Root that Names the Whole

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body of presence: the screen laid down, the eyes lifted to another's eyes, the ear turned to the one who waits. He does not parse letters; he feels what it is to be truly here, and that the deepest thing between people is unseen, like a root. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)
j-dh-rjadhr · judhūr · tajadhdhara · mutajadhdhir · judhayr
❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word jadhr (root) has siblings! The many branching origins beneath the earth are judhūr (roots); a tree that has fixed and settled in the earth has tajadhdhara; a deep, firm, settled thing is mutajadhdhir; and a small root is a judhayr. All one family from one father: j-dh-r.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (j-dh-r) is sound. Its core sense is the origin and base of a thing. From it come the root of a tree (its origin beneath the earth) and the root of a word (the origin it branches from, the heart of the Juthur system). Remind the teacher that what lies beneath the earth is often greater than what rises above it, as a person's hidden origin is deeper than his outward show.

The Full Telling: “The Roots of a Table”

For reading and reflection in the family · seven minutes · with five original plates in the harvest palette

I. The silent supper: a full table, absent hearts, and Grandmother Ramla watching in silence

After a long day's effort, the weary mother gathered her scattered family around the supper table. She had made each one what he loved: for this one his favourite dish, for that one her favourite sweet, and for the little one the pizza he adores, and he was the last to come, for he wished to finish something “important” first.

They sat. But the table was silent. The elder brother was finishing his game, the sister was messaging her friends, the father was following a news item, and all of them, strangely, hid their phones beneath the table, stealing glances at them as if they were a secret. Present in body, absent in heart. And Grandmother Ramla looked at them in silence, a quiet sorrow in her eyes.

II. The silent wound: a child holds his drawing, waiting to be seen, and meets a pretended care

The little one was carrying something precious: a drawing he had made that day in class. His teacher had told him it was the loveliest drawing in the class! So he had waited for this moment, the supper, when the family is gathered, to surprise them with it, for it is the only time he finds them together.

He lifted his drawing and said with joy: “Look what I drew!” But… no one raised his eyes. One of them said without looking, “Lovely, my dear,” and went back to his screen. They only mimed their care, their hearts far away. The little one laid his drawing quietly on his lap, and the light in his eyes dimmed. He was not angry, only sad, for he had wanted them to see him, and no one saw.

III. An innocent question breaks the ice of silence, and the heads and smiles rise

The little one looked around him: everyone bent over a lap, and the light of the screens dancing on their faces. So he thought with his child's logic, then asked, in his small voice, a question that made them all laugh:

“Mama… are the phones hungry too? You all feed them and look at them all the time… I'm the only hungry one, but I'm hungry for you, not for the food!”

The father laughed, the sister smiled, the brother lifted his head. The innocent question broke the ice of silence, and did what lectures cannot: it touched the hearts with honesty, not with blame. They looked at one another, as if they had just woken.

IV. The bread basket gathers what the screens had stolen: a loaf for each, and the phone in the basket

And suddenly, the little one rose, and took up the bread basket. He carried it round to everyone, and said as if it were a beloved game:

“This is a game! I give each of you a piece of bread… in exchange for putting a phone in the basket! Bread is eaten together, and the phone can wait.”

They all laughed and agreed. Each took a piece of bread and placed a phone in the basket. And so the basket that had carried the bread, which gathers us, came now to carry the phones, which steal our presence when we misuse them. And for the first time in a long while, the hands were emptied of screens, and the table filled with faces.

V. The drawing and the root: a tree (Noor) with a little root, a question that opens the doors, and a mother's tear

Now that the hearts had returned, the father, who a moment before had been the least attentive, said: “And now, my little one, tell us about your drawing.” The child's face lit, and he lifted his drawing: a lush and lovely tree, and beneath it, below the line of the earth, a little root, drawn in childlike strokes.

And he began to tell, in his small words, what the teacher had said: “Today she challenged us to draw what we cannot see! She said: do you see the tree in front of the class? Draw it with no leaves and no fruit… draw what is under the earth, she meant the roots! And she said: a tree cannot live without the part no one sees; it is the unknown soldier, the secret of survival we never notice, yet it gives the tree its firmness, its life, and its lofty voice… and it works in silence.”

The table fell silent, but this time it was the silence of reflection, not of distraction. Then the child lifted his eyes, and asked a question that shook the hearts:

“And I was thinking… does a person have a root, like a tree? Is there something in us that is the secret of our lasting, but we cannot see it?”

No one answered in words. But the mother's eyes filled with tears, not from sorrow, but because her youngest had said what she, for all her effort, could not. And each looked at those around him, as if seeing his roots for the first time. And the drawing lay at the centre of the table: a tree named Noor, and beneath it a small root, the secret of all that grew.

And in that moment they all understood, without anyone saying a thing, that the hidden root of their family was this very moment: to gather, and to listen, and to see what lies beneath the words. They were gathered… no longer apart, though they had been present all along. And on the table the drawing seemed to pulse: a tree bearing a fruit, and in the heart of the fruit a small seed, waiting… to begin the story anew.

And it was as though Noor: who began, in the first book, as a small seed learning to breathe, had become today a fruiting tree, living on in this child who was present in his heart, and so woke the hearts. And he, in his turn, carries a new seed… so Noor does not end, but is born again in every child who is present, and listens, and asks, and speaks with the heart to those around him. For presence is the first manner of dialogue, and all expression begins with our being truly here.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Sit a moment, and imagine a supper table where everyone is present in body, absent in heart. What is the hidden root that ties your hearts? And when was the last time you looked into the eyes of one you love, with no screen between you?”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

The same tale, in shorter sentences.

The weary mother gathered her family for supper and made each one what he loved, but all were busy with the phone beneath the table, glancing at it in secret.

The little one held up his drawing, the loveliest in the class, to show them, and no one raised his eyes, and his light dimmed.

He asked innocently: “Are the phones hungry? I'm the one who's hungry · for you!” They laughed and woke, then he gathered their phones in the bread basket in exchange for a piece of bread each.

He told of the teacher's challenge, “draw what you cannot see: the root,” and asked: “Is there a hidden root in us, the secret of our lasting?” The mother's eyes filled, and the hearts gathered without parting.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. A fruit that bears a seed (beginning): turns to the one who awaits his care when reminded, and is present in heart for moments. A fruit that bears a seed (mastery): is present in heart, not in body only, sees the hidden root in his bonds, and passes on what he learned to the one who comes after, so his fruit bears a new seed.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR C1 / ACTFL Advanced Low). The child can be present in heart with another (looking, listening, setting distraction aside), narrate and reflect on an affective situation, perceive the unseen root beneath the surface of people and things, and teach a skill he has mastered to another, turning a learner into a teacher.

CASEL Behavioural Criteria (integrative · the journey gathered)

  • Is present in heart in his meetings (he looks, he listens, he sets distractions aside).
  • Notices the one who awaits his care and turns to him sincerely.
  • Sees beneath the surface: the hidden root in people and things.
  • Passes on what he learned to another, so every fruit becomes a new seed.

The Bread Basket & What We Cannot See

Two small, tangible rituals carry the whole meaning of this book: one for the table, one for the heart.

✦ The Bread Basket
At the meal, each places his phone in one basket and takes a piece of bread. Bread is eaten together; the phone waits. The basket that gathers us carries away what scatters us.
✦ Draw the Root
Each draws the tree of his family, and beneath the earth-line, the hidden root that binds them (love, memories, moments together). Draw what cannot be seen.
❦  The lesson. The greatest roots are the most hidden, and the truest presence is to see the one before you. In the Arabic master: «مَا لَا نَرَاهُ، قَدْ يَكُونُ أَهَمَّ مَا فِينَا» · what we cannot see may be the most important thing in us.

The Four Inner Senses of Presence

Presence, too, is felt in the body before it is a skill. CRT awakens the child's interoception, the inner sense of being truly here. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in presenceA guiding whisper
The laid-down screenThe lightness of the freed, empty hand“Feel your hand rest empty and open · nothing between you and them.”
The lifted gazeThe meeting when the eyes rise to another's eyes“Lift your eyes to his eyes · feel the meeting there.”
The turned earThe attention that leans toward the one who waits“Turn your whole self to the one who waits · feel him seen.”
The unseen rootThe sense of the deep thing beneath the words“Feel the root beneath, the love that no one sees, and everyone needs.”
❦  How to use these whispers. Do them at the table, with every phone in the basket first. Stay with the unseen root longest, and let no one be hurried or blamed.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. This book is a tender wakefulness, not a trial. We do not turn it into blame of the family or a demonizing of technology (the screen has its good; the trouble is only when it absents the present). We let no child feel that his family does not love him; we open hearts gently. Legacy is a love passed on, not a guilt passed on.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Autism spectrum (ASD)Eye contact may be hard; accept presence of the heart in other forms (sitting together, listening, sharing food) without requiring a look in the eyes.
A busy familyOffer the model gently, without blame, and begin with a small step: one screen-free meal a week.
A child who feels truly neglectedSurround him with warmth, reassure him that the grown-ups' busyness is not because they do not love him, and guide him to one he trusts if the pain endures.
An over-digital environmentTeach balance, not deprivation; the screen has its time, and presence has its time, and the household sets the example.
Language difficultyAccept presence expressed by a deed (sharing food, a drawing, an embrace, sitting together), not by words alone.
Cultural differenceRespect the differing conventions of the table and of gathering across families and cultures.
A child with no table to gather atThe table here is symbolic. A child with no table (an orphan, or one whose family works nights or lives far away, or who is in alternative care) is invited into any moment of gathering he loves: a cup of tea with a grandfather, the walk to school with a friend, a video call. He is never asked in front of others: where is your table?

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the presence basket. Place your phones in one basket, like the child's basket in the story, for the whole session, and make it a habit at every table.

ii. During the session · draw “what we cannot see.” Ask each one to draw or write the “root” of his family: what binds you that cannot be seen? Then share what you drew, and reflect on how much of the root works in silence.

iii. After the session · the “I see you” moment. Give each child, each day, one moment of full, screen-free attention: look into his eyes, and ask, “How was your day, really?” The child who is seen learns to see others.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many meals did we gather with no screens this week?” A star for each heart-present meal. Count the stars at week's end.

“The screen brings the absent near and absents the present. We choose to be present for those we love.”

The Juthur Scale (JPS) · Book Twenty Unit

❧  Important. The Juthur Scale is a pedagogical observation tool under development · not a psychological or medical diagnosis. Its readings are advisory and pass no verdict upon the child.
❦  How the measure grows · and completes. With this closing book all the dimensions of the Juthur Scale (JPS) are complete, from the breath to the counsel. And the closing dimension gathers them: presence of the heart and the passing-on of what we learned.

Filled twice, by two independent raters where possible; if not, one direct observer suffices. The marks are behaviourally anchored:

MarkBehavioural anchor (what you actually observe)
○  rarelyPresent in body, absent in heart; does not notice the one who awaits his care.
◐  sometimesIs present in heart only when reminded; does not yet see the hidden root or pass on what he learned.
●  oftenUnprompted, is present in heart, sees the hidden root in his bonds, and passes on what he learned to another.

The rating procedure, the rule for resolving differences between raters, and the research status of the scale (exploratory, testable, never diagnostic) are set out once, in Book I.

Questions of Understanding

  • Why did the little one wait for the supper to show his family the drawing? recall
  • Why did the child's innocent question change the table more than lectures and advice? inference
  • Imagine your table now. Who is waiting for you to truly see him? What would you say to him? sensory
  • What is the hidden root in your life, the thing you do not see, but that is the secret of your lasting and your firmness? personal link · existential
  • No one at the table was harsh. What was each of them fleeing to his phone to avoid? And how do we help them without blame? empathy for the other
  • How do we make a moment of presence for someone who has no table? And whom in your life will you invite to a moment of gathering this week? application
  • What one skill from this whole journey will you pass on, and to whom? legacy

Craft, Media & the Closing Project

The drawing. Keep the family's “Draw the Root” picture on the wall for a week, and let each person add one new root a day.

The family deed. Keep the Bread Basket for a whole week of meals, then tell one another: what did you discover in each other's faces? A phone goes into the basket in place of a piece of bread, for the whole meal.

The Closing Project · Design Your Own Book

You are now an author, no longer only a reader. Choose one skill you mastered on the journey (a breath, a voice, a dialogue, a wisdom…), then make “your own Book Twenty-One”: invent a linguistic root, a hero, a short story, and one sensory anchor the reader can try with his body, then teach it to someone younger than you. Show your book to someone you trust before you share it, and name no one in it and use no one's picture without permission. And so the circle closes: you have become a seed that sows a seed, a learner become a teacher.

Read it aloud with no screen, which is lovelier and more lasting. In a book that lifts up presence, the screen is the last of the means, not the first.
