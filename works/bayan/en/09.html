The Single Thread · presence, made visible. The melody gave the voice its feeling; now the gesture gives the whole body a voice, hands that point, a face that shows, a self expressed in sound and body at once. Melody is the music of the voice; gesture is the music of the body, together they are the complete eloquence. It reaches back to the melody and forward to true dialogue.

Before You Begin · for Teacher & Parent

❧  Proactive caution. This book grows body language. Some children (especially on the autism spectrum) may find reading gestures hard. Teach the signals explicitly and gently.
❦  How to use this book. Two tellings: a brief telling (3 minutes) and a full telling (7 minutes). The tale continues: Noor the branch tells with melody, and now learns to let her body accompany her voice.

The Heart of the Book

Noor learned melody, so her voice carried her feelings. But she discovered that speech is not voice alone; it is body too. The hands point and the face expresses, and a speaker rigid as a statue loses half his meaning.

❦  Historical footnote. Al-Jahiz (Amr ibn Baḥr, d. 255 AH), a great man of letters of Basra who wrote in Arabic, author of al-Bayān wa'l-Tabyīn, taught that eloquence (bayān) is of several kinds: the spoken word, the gesture, the reckoning on the fingers, the written line, and the state of things themselves. So the gesture stands beside the spoken word. He said of it: “The word and the gesture are partners, and how good a helper it is to it” («وَاللَّفْظُ وَالْإِشَارَةُ شَرِيكَانِ، وَنِعْمَ الْعَوْنُ هِيَ لَهُ»). From him we draw that the body is a language. What al-Jahiz says in the story is the author's own composition, apart from the words quoted from him in al-Bayān wa'l-Tabyīn; Grandmother Ramla is a fictional character.

The Root & Its Kin · a Skill and a Language

❧  Body before symbol (the first CRT principle). The child meets this root through the body itself: hands that open for “big,” a face that lifts with surprise. He does not parse letters; he gestures, and feels his meaning completed by his body. The morphology below is for the teacher alone.
Root (the skill)Root family (words of the same lineage)
w-m-ʾīmāʾ · yūmiʾu · mūmiʾ · mūmaʾ ilayh · awmaʾa
❦  How to explain it to the child. “The word īmāʾ (gesture) has siblings! One who points with the hand yūmiʾu; a single sign is an īmāʾa; and when you signalled your friend to come near, you awmaʾta to him. One family from one father: w-m-ʾ.”
❦  Morphological note for the teacher. The root (w-m-ʾ) is initial-weak and final-hamzated: its first radical is a wāw and its last a hamza. The verb “awmaʾa” follows the pattern “afʿala” and its present “yūmiʾu” follows “yufʿilu”, so the first radical, the wāw, lengthens into a long vowel (ū, ī) in these forms; the hamza is the final radical, visible in awmaʾa, īmāʾa and yūmiʾu.

The Full Telling: “Noor's Gesture”

For reading aloud with the child · seven minutes · with five original plates in the branch palette

I. Noor tells with a lovely melody, but her body is rigid, with no gesture

Noor came to tell with a lovely melody that carried her feelings. But she told with her boughs held straight and stiff, her face unmoving, like a painting hung on a wall. The voice was expressive, but the body was utterly silent.

Noor noticed her tale seemed incomplete. When she said, “I saw a huge bird!” she did not open her arms to show its size, so the bird stayed small in the listener's imagination. What she lacked was to let her body speak.

II. The Rigid Statue speaks with no motion at all

And in the garden there stood the Rigid Statue. It talked a great deal, but never moved a hand or a feature, hard as stone, one face however much it said. It stood so still because it feared that any movement would be laughed at. It told Noor in a flat voice:

“Motion is folly, Noor! Stand still like me, and move nothing. Hands are for lifting, not for speaking; the face is for looking, not for expressing!”

Noor tried the Statue's way: she told a tale while utterly still. Her tale seemed cold and far, and no one felt it. People love to see the meaning, not only to hear it. Stillness veiled half her heart.

III. Al-Jahiz and Grandmother Ramla teach Noor that the body is a language

Beside the soil sat the man of letters al-Jahiz, who wrote that eloquence is of kinds: a word, a gesture, a line, a state of things. And with him Grandmother Ramla, whom we know from before. Al-Jahiz moved his hands as he spoke:

“Noor, look how my hands speak with me! When I say big, I open my arms. When I say small, I bring my fingers close. A gesture is the word's partner, so make it its helper.”

And Grandmother Ramla added: “You are a branch, Noor, and a branch has boughs that sway with the wind! Let your two boughs speak with your voice, and your face express.”

IV. Noor moves her boughs and face, expressing with her tale

Noor began to tell anew with her whole body! When she said “a huge bird” she flung her two boughs wide open. When she said “it vanished suddenly” she drew them in fast. Her brows lifted with surprise, and she smiled with joy.

And a lovely thing happened: the listeners lived the tale as if they were seeing it! Noor now spoke with voice and body together, and her eloquence was complete, and her meaning arrived whole, to the hearts and the eyes.

V. Noor speaks with voice and body; the Statue moves and smiles

Noor came to tell with her whole being: a tuned voice, hands that point, a face that expresses. She said, waving her bough with life: “Come, look what I found!” And everyone hurried to her.

The Statue had been watching Noor, and now it tried… and its arm moved a little! Then a smile appeared on its stone face. It said, almost in a whisper: “I was rigid, so I seemed far; now I have moved… and I am near, and alive!”

And the Statue learned that a moving, expressive body draws hearts near. Noor went on telling with voice and body together, and everyone who saw her tell came to love her.

❦  The Silence of the Bodily Question (4–5 seconds). “Tell the one beside you about something big, with your hands only and no words. Did they understand? The body speaks!”

The Brief Telling (for the youngest · 3 minutes)

Short sentences, the same meaning.

Noor came to tell with a lovely melody, but her body was rigid, so her tale seemed incomplete.

The Rigid Statue spoke without motion; Noor copied it, and seemed cold and far.

Al-Jahiz said, moving his hands: “A gesture is eloquence like a word! Let your boughs speak.”

Noor told with voice and body: “Come, look!” The listeners lived her tale, and the Statue moved and smiled.

Sub-Degree & Mastery Criterion (graduated)

A two-stage criterion. An extending branch (beginning): uses one gesture with his speech, with a reminder. ← An extending branch (mastery): tells using his hands and face naturally to confirm the meaning unprompted, and reads another's gesture.

❧  CRT Can-Do (a communicative criterion; CEFR A2 / ACTFL Intermediate Low). The child can accompany his words with fitting hand-gestures and facial expression to complete his meaning, and can read a partner's gesture and respond to it.

CASEL Behavioral Criteria (Relationship & Expression)

  • Uses his hands to point and express while speaking.
  • Expresses with his face what he feels.
  • Reads another's facial expressions and gestures.
  • Matches his words to the motion of his body.

The Four Inner Senses of Gesture

Gesture, too, is felt in the body before it is a skill. CRT awakens the child's interoception of movement. Guide the child to notice, without naming the terms:

Inner senseWhat the child feels in gestureA guiding whisper
The reach (the open hand)The stretch of the arms opening for something big“Feel your arms open wide when the thing is big.”
The face's moveBrows lifting with surprise, a smile with joy“Let your eyebrows and your smile join the words.”
Match (voice and body together)The rightness when word and motion agree“Do your hand and your word say the same thing?”
Reading the otherSensing a friend's feeling from his body“What is your friend's body telling you, without words?”
❦  Why this matters. A child who feels the reach, the face's move, the match, and the reading of others speaks with his whole self, and understands others before a single word is said.

Safe-Stop Protocol & Inclusion

❧  Safe-stop protocol. If the child is embarrassed to move, he is not forced. Gesture is grown by play, not by command. What matters is communication, not performance.
ConditionSuggested alternative
Autism spectrum (ASD)Teach the basic gestures explicitly with pictures; facial expression is never forced.
Motor difficultyUse whatever is available (a nod of the head, a movement of the eyes); gesture is adapted to the child's ability.
Visual impairmentFocus on audible and tactile gesture, and describe another's gesture in words.
Shyness / anxietyBegin gesture in a mime game with one companion before the group.
HyperactivityChannel the movement energy into purposeful, expressive gesture instead of random motion.
Cultural differenceRespect that gestures differ across cultures; a polite sign in one place may be rude in another, so teach the meaning, not one fixed form. Never ask a child to make a sign that carries a different meaning at home.

Guide for Parent & Teacher

i. Before the session · the screen-box. Put devices away. Before a screen the child's body is still; we revive the language of his whole body.

ii. During the session · the mime game. The child expresses a word (elephant, sleeping, joy) with his body alone, and the others guess.

iii. After the session · tell with your hands. Encourage the child to tell his day using his hands and face.

❦  Make it measurable. Record on the fridge ledger: “How many times did our child use his hands or face to express?” A star for each. Count the stars at week's end.

“The screen freezes the child's body. We make his body speak.”

English Bridge: “My body speaks with my voice, and so my meaning is complete.” A word with a gesture is a word that is truly seen.

Questions of Understanding

  • Why did the bird stay small in the listener's imagination? recall
  • Why did the Statue seem cold and far, though it spoke? inference
  • Show “big” and then “small” with your hands only. How did your body speak? sensory
  • Have you ever felt eager to show something and shy to move your hands, at the same moment? simultaneous emotions
  • The Statue stayed still because it feared to move. Have you ever held your body stiff to hide a feeling? What could help the Statue let its body speak? empathy for the other
  • When did a person's gesture help you understand them more than their words? personal link
My speech is voice and body together: the hand and the face complete what the tongue says.

❦  Behavioral translation (not a slogan only). Teach the child: “When you tell a tale, let your hands point and your face express. Smile with joy, open your hands with surprise.” By gesture, Noor's eloquence was completed.

Craft & Media

The drawing. Ask the child to draw faces with different feelings (joy, surprise, sorrow), then mimic them before a mirror.

The play. Children play “Statue and Life”: they freeze like statues, and at a signal express a word with their bodies. Whose body is most eloquent?

Listening. There is no recording of this book yet. Read the tale aloud, slowly, and keep the pauses. The Listening room gathers the recordings that exist.

To give children a calm place beside the screens, and give the child back the language of his whole living body.

Noor can tell her tale with melody now, but she tells it with her boughs held stiff and her face still, so her meaning stays half-veiled. When the Rigid Statue urges her to move nothing, a great man of letters, who taught that a gesture is eloquence like a word, and a grandmother show her how to open her boughs for “big,” draw them close for “small,” and let her face express. A tangible, sensory tale in the tradition of Concrete Reading Theory: no still screen-body, only the child, speaking with all of himself.
